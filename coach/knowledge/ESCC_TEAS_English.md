## Spelling

**TEAS objective E.1.1** · Conventions of Standard English

**What the TEAS expects:** you can spot the correctly spelled word, choose between commonly confused words (their/there/they're, affect/effect), and apply common spelling rules.

---

### 1. Rules that cover most words

A **suffix** is an ending added to a word (*-ing, -ed, -ful, -ness*); a **prefix** is a beginning added to a word (*un-, re-*). Many spelling rules are about what happens when you add a suffix.

| Rule | Examples | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **i before e, except after c** (or when it sounds like "ay") | believe, field; receive, ceiling; neighbor, weigh | Exceptions: weird, seize, either, neither, foreign |
| **Drop the silent e** before a suffix that starts with a vowel | hope → hoping; care → caring | Keep the e before a consonant suffix: hopeful, careless |
| **Change y to i** when a consonant comes before the y | happy → happiness; carry → carried | Keep the y before -ing (carrying) or after a vowel (played) |
| **Double the final consonant** before a vowel suffix when the word ends in one vowel + one consonant **and** the last syllable is stressed (all one-syllable words count) | stop → stopped; run → running; occur → occurrence; begin → beginning | Don't double if the stress isn't on the last syllable (benefit → benefited, open → opened) or for two vowels (rain → raining), or for words ending in w, x, or y (fix → fixing, snow → snowing) |
| **Plurals:** add -es after s, x, z, ch, sh | boxes, churches, wishes | Consonant + y → -ies (baby → babies) |
| **Irregular (often Latin or Greek) plurals** | diagnosis → diagnoses; bacterium → bacteria; vertebra → vertebrae; nucleus → nuclei | Learn these by sight; they're common in health courses |

---

### 2. Commonly confused words

| Words | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|
| **their / there / they're** | their = belongs to them; there = a place; they're = they are |
| **your / you're** | your = belongs to you; you're = you are |
| **its / it's** | its = belongs to it; it's = it is |
| **affect / effect** | **A**ffect is usually the **A**ction (a verb: to influence); **E**ffect is usually the **E**nd result (a noun). Test: after *a, an, the*, or an adjective, use **effect** |
| **then / than** | then = time; than = comparison |
| **accept / except** | accept = receive or agree to; except = but, excluding |
| **to / too / two** | to = toward (go to class) or before a verb (to study); too = also or excessive; two = 2 |
| **whose / who's** | whose = belongs to whom; who's = who is |
| **lose / loose** | lose = misplace or fail to win; loose = not tight |
| **breath / breathe** | breath = noun (take a breath); breathe = verb (breathe deeply) |

**Test for contractions:** replace the word with the two words it stands for. "It's raining" → "It is raining" ✓. "The dog wagged it's tail" → "it is tail" ✗, so use **its**.

> **Check 1:** Choose the correct word: "The medication had a strong ___ on her blood pressure." (affect / effect)
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Words often misspelled

**accommodate** (two c's, two m's) · **necessary** (one c, two s's) · **separate** ("a rat" in separate) · **definitely** · **occurrence** (two r's) · **privilege** · **receive** · **schedule** · **benefited** · **embarrass** (two r's, two s's)

> **Check 2:** Which is spelled correctly: occurence, occurrence, ocurrence?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Trusting the "sound" of a word.** Many errors come from spelling words the way they're pronounced.
- **Putting an apostrophe in "its" for possession.** Possessive pronouns (its, yours, theirs) never take apostrophes.
- **Using "then" in comparisons.** "Taller than," not "taller then."

---

### Learn it properly

---

### Check answers

1. **effect.** It follows "a strong," so a noun is needed.
2. **occurrence.** Double c and double r.

## Punctuation

**TEAS objective E.1.2** · Conventions of Standard English

**What the TEAS expects:** you can use commas, semicolons, colons, apostrophes, and quotation marks correctly.

---

> **Words you need**
> - **Clause:** a group of words with a subject and a verb.
> - **Independent clause:** a clause that can stand alone as a sentence. *The test was long.*
> - **Dependent clause:** a clause that starts with a word like *because, when, although, if*, or *since* and can't stand alone. *Because the test was long*
> - **Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS):** the seven joining words **for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so**.

---

### 1. Commas

| Use a comma… | Example |
|---|---|
| After an **introductory** word, phrase, or clause | After the shift ended, the nurse wrote her notes. |
| Between items in a **series** | Bring your ID, two pencils, and a water bottle. |
| Before a FANBOYS word (**for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so**) joining two complete sentences | The test was long, but she finished on time. |
| Around **extra information** that could be removed without changing who or what you mean | Dr. Lee, the new surgeon, starts Monday. |
| In **dates and addresses** | May 4, 2026, in Norfolk, Virginia |

**The serial (Oxford) comma**, before "and" in a list, is standard in academic writing, and these pages always use it. Some styles (such as newspapers) leave it out; whichever you choose, be consistent.

**Don't put a comma:**
- between a subject and its verb ("The nurse, wrote" ✗);
- between two complete sentences with no joining word (a **comma splice**);
- around information you **need** to know which one is meant: *The nurse who works nights is here* (no commas: "who works nights" tells you which nurse). Clauses starting with **that** never take commas; clauses starting with **which** usually do.

> **Check 1:** Add the needed comma: "Because the clinic was busy the patients waited."
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Semicolons and colons

- **Semicolon (;):** joins two complete sentences that are closely related. *I studied every night; I felt ready.* Also separates list items that already contain commas.
- **However, therefore, for example:** when one of these words connects two complete sentences, put a **semicolon before it and a comma after it**: *I studied every night; therefore, I felt ready.* A comma alone before "however" makes a comma splice: "I studied, however I felt nervous" ✗.
- **Colon (:):** introduces a list, explanation, or example, **after a complete sentence**. *Bring three things: your ID, a pencil, and water.* Not after "such as" or a verb: "Bring: your ID" ✗.

---

### 3. Apostrophes

| Use | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Singular possessive | add **'s** (even if the word ends in s) | the patient's chart; the boss's office |
| Plural possessive (ends in s) | add **'** | the patients' charts |
| Plural possessive (no s) | add **'s** | the children's toys |
| Contraction | apostrophe replaces missing letters | do not → don't |

**Never** use an apostrophe to make a plain plural ("three patient's" ✗) or with possessive pronouns (its, yours, hers, theirs).

> **Check 2:** Which is correct for the charts of several nurses: nurse's charts, nurses' charts, nurses's charts?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 4. Quotation marks

In American English, **periods and commas go inside** closing quotation marks: *She said, "I'm ready."* **Colons and semicolons go outside.** Question marks go inside only if the quotation itself is a question.

---

### 5. Capitalization

Capitalize:
- The **first word** of a sentence and of a full-sentence quotation. *She said, "We start at noon."*
- **Proper nouns:** names of specific people, places, organizations, days, months, and holidays. *Dr. Lee, Norfolk, Eastern Shore Community College, Monday, July*
- **Titles before a name** (*Doctor Patel*), but not alone (*the doctor arrived*).
- **Main words in titles** of books and articles. *Writing Guide with Handbook*

Don't capitalize **seasons** (spring), general subjects (biology, but **English** and **Spanish**, which are languages), or directions (drive north) unless they name a region (the South).

---

### Common traps

- **Comma splices.** Two sentences need a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a FANBOYS word.
- **"However" with only commas** between two sentences. Use a semicolon before it.
- **Apostrophes in plurals.** "Patients" (more than one) has no apostrophe.
- **Colons after incomplete sentences.**

---

### Learn it properly

- **Grammar, punctuation, and style handbook:** OpenStax *Writing Guide with Handbook*, Handbook: https://openstax.org/books/writing-guide/pages/handbook

---

### Check answers

1. **Because the clinic was busy, the patients waited.** "Because the clinic was busy" is an introductory dependent clause, so a comma follows it.
2. **nurses' charts.** Plural noun ending in s: add only an apostrophe.

## Sentence Structure

**TEAS objective E.1.3** · Conventions of Standard English

**What the TEAS expects:** you can recognize complete sentences, fix fragments and run-ons, identify sentence types, and place modifiers correctly.

---

### 1. What makes a complete sentence

A complete sentence (an **independent clause**) has a **subject** (who or what the sentence is about), a **verb** (the action or state), and expresses a **complete thought**.

A **dependent clause** also has a subject and verb, but it starts with a word like *because, when, although, if*, or *since*, so it can't stand alone. (See the "Words you need" box on the E.1.2 Punctuation page.)

- **Fragment:** missing a subject or verb, or begins with a word like *because, when, although, if, since* without finishing the thought. *Because the clinic was closed.* ✗
- **Not a fragment:** a command such as *Sit down.* The subject "you" is understood.
- **Fix:** attach it to a complete sentence. *Because the clinic was closed, we went home.* ✓

---

### 2. Run-ons and comma splices

- **Run-on:** two complete sentences with no punctuation. *The results came back they were normal.* ✗
- **Comma splice:** two complete sentences joined by only a comma. *The results came back, they were normal.* ✗
- **Four fixes:**
  1. Period: *The results came back. They were normal.*
  2. Semicolon: *The results came back; they were normal.*
  3. Comma + a FANBOYS word (**for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so**): *The results came back, and they were normal.*
  4. Make one clause dependent: *When the results came back, they were normal.*

> **Check 1:** Is this correct? "I finished the test, I felt relieved."
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Four sentence types

| Type | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | One independent clause (may have one subject doing two actions) | The nurse checked the chart and called the doctor. |
| **Compound** | Two independent clauses joined by a comma + FANBOYS word or a semicolon | The nurse checked the chart, and the doctor reviewed it. |
| **Complex** | One independent + at least one dependent clause | Because she checked the chart, the nurse knew the dose. |
| **Compound-complex** | Two independent + at least one dependent clause | When the lab called, the nurse answered, and the doctor listened. |

**Tip:** first count the **independent clauses** (ones that could stand alone), then look for a **dependent clause** (one starting with *because, when, although, if, since*).
- "The nurse checked … and called": **one** subject with two verbs, so one clause: **simple**.
- "The nurse checked the chart, and the doctor reviewed it": two clauses that could each stand alone: **compound**.
- "Because she checked the chart, the nurse knew the dose": the first clause can't stand alone: **complex**.

---

### 4. Modifiers

A **modifier** is a word or phrase that describes something else (*covered in mud, almost, walking into the room*). It should sit **next to** the word it describes.
- **Dangling modifier:** *Walking into the room, the monitor beeped.* (The monitor wasn't walking.) ✗
- **Fixed:** *Walking into the room, the nurse heard the monitor beep.* ✓
- **Misplaced modifier:** *She almost drove the patient for an hour.* → *She drove the patient for almost an hour.*

> **Check 2:** Fix: "Covered in mud, she washed the dog."
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 5. Parallel structure

Items in a list or pair should have the same grammatical form.
*She likes reading, swimming, and to hike.* ✗ → *She likes reading, swimming, and hiking.* ✓

---

### Common traps

- **Calling a long sentence a run-on.** Length doesn't matter; joining is what matters.
- **Mistaking one subject with two verbs for a compound sentence.** It needs two complete clauses.
- **Starting with a phrase that describes the wrong noun.**

---

### Learn it properly

- **Grammar, punctuation, and style handbook:** OpenStax *Writing Guide with Handbook*, Handbook: https://openstax.org/books/writing-guide/pages/handbook

---

### Check answers

1. **No: it's a comma splice.** Fix: "I finished the test, and I felt relieved," or use a period or semicolon.
2. Any version that puts the mud on the dog works, for example: **"She washed the dog, which was covered in mud,"** or "She washed the mud-covered dog."

## Grammar for Clear Writing

**TEAS objective E.2.1** · Knowledge of Language

**What the TEAS expects:** you can make subjects and verbs agree, choose the right pronoun, keep verb tense consistent, and avoid unclear pronoun references.

---

> **Words you need**
> - **Subject:** who or what the sentence is about. **Verb:** the action or state (*runs, is, seems*).
> - **Pronoun:** a word that stands in for a noun (*she, it, they, him*). Its **antecedent** is the noun it stands for: in *The nurses brought their badges*, "nurses" is the antecedent of "their."
> - **Preposition:** a small word that shows position or relationship (*of, in, on, between, to, with, for*).
> - **First person** = the speaker (*I, we*); **second person** = the one spoken to (*you*); **third person** = everyone else (*he, she, it, they*).

---

### 1. Subject–verb agreement

Singular subject → singular verb; plural subject → plural verb.

| Tricky case | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Words between subject and verb | Ignore them; find the true subject | The **box** of gloves **is** empty. |
| each, every, either, neither, anyone, everybody (used alone) | **Singular** | **Each** of the students **has** a badge. **Neither** of the doors **is** locked. |
| Subjects joined by **and** | Plural | Maria and Jo **are** here. |
| Pairs with **or / nor** (either…or, neither…nor) | Verb agrees with the subject **nearer to it** | Neither the nurses nor the **doctor was** late. Neither the doctor nor the **nurses were** late. |
| **some, all, none, most, any** | Depends on the noun after "of" | **Some** of the **water is** gone. **Some** of the **cups are** gone. (Some tests still treat *none* as always singular: *None of the cups is…*) |
| Collective nouns (team, staff, class) | Usually singular in American English | The **staff is** meeting at noon. |
| Sentences starting with **There is / There are** | The subject comes **after** the verb | There **is** one **chart** on the desk. There **are** two **charts**. |

> **Check 1:** Choose the verb: "The list of patients (is / are) on the desk."
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Pronoun case

| | Subject form (does the action) | Object form (receives the action, or follows a preposition) |
|---|---|---|
| First person | I, we | me, us |
| Second person | you | you |
| Third person | he, she, they | him, her, them |
| Questions | who | whom |

- **Test by removing the other person:** "Maria and (I / me) went." → "I went" ✓.
- After prepositions use object forms: "between you and **me**."
- **Myself** must refer back to *I* or *me* earlier in the sentence: "I hurt myself," "I did it myself." It can't replace *I* or *me*: "Maria and myself went" ✗.
- **Who or whom?** Answer the question with he or him. *(Who/Whom) called?* → **He** called → **who**. *(Who/Whom) did you call?* → I called **him** → **whom**.

---

### 3. Pronoun–antecedent agreement and clarity

- A pronoun must **match its antecedent in number**: one noun → singular pronoun; more than one → plural. *The **nurses** brought **their** badges.* *The **nurse** brought **her** badge.*
- A pronoun must clearly refer to one noun. *When Dana called Kim, she was upset.* (Who was upset?) ✗ → *Dana was upset when she called Kim.* ✓
- Singular "they" is now widely accepted for a person whose gender is unknown or who uses they: *Each student should bring their ID.* Some formal styles still prefer *his or her*. On a test, the safest choice is often an answer that makes the subject plural so "their" clearly fits: *Students should bring their IDs.*

---

### 4. Consistent verb tense

Don't shift tense without a reason. *She opened the chart and reads the notes.* ✗ → *She opened the chart and read the notes.* ✓

> **Check 2:** Fix: "Yesterday the nurse checks the IV and called the doctor."
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 5. Adjectives vs adverbs

Adjectives describe nouns; adverbs describe verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs. *She did* ***well*** *on the test* (not *good*). *The patient feels* ***bad*** (not *badly*). **Linking verbs** (*is, seems, feels, looks, becomes*) connect the subject to a word that describes it, so they take an adjective. Test: if you can swap in "is" (*The patient is bad*), use the adjective.

---

### 6. Clear, direct sentences

- **Active voice** (the subject does the action) is usually clearer than **passive voice** (the subject receives it). *The nurse gave the medication* (active) vs *The medication was given by the nurse* (passive). Passive is acceptable when the doer is unknown or unimportant, as in many lab reports.
- **Cut wordiness:** *due to the fact that* → *because*; *at this point in time* → *now*; *in order to* → *to*.
- **Fewer vs less:** *fewer* for things you can count (fewer patients); *less* for amounts (less water).

---

### Common traps

- **Matching the verb to the nearest noun** instead of the subject.
- **"Me and her went."** Use subject forms in the subject.
- **Unclear "it," "this," or "they."** Name the noun if two could fit.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Grammar, punctuation, and style handbook:** OpenStax *Writing Guide with Handbook*, Handbook: https://openstax.org/books/writing-guide/pages/handbook

---

### Check answers

1. **is.** The subject is "list" (singular); "of patients" is a phrase in between.
2. **Yesterday the nurse checked the IV and called the doctor.** Both verbs in past tense.

## Writing for Your Audience

**TEAS objective E.2.2** · Knowledge of Language

**What the TEAS expects:** you can judge whether wording fits its purpose and audience, choosing formal or informal language and plain or technical terms as the situation requires.

---

### 1. Formal vs informal

| Formal (essays, reports, emails to instructors or employers) | Informal (texts and notes to friends) |
|---|---|
| Complete sentences, standard spelling | Fragments, abbreviations (u, pls, ASAP) |
| Precise words: *support, demonstrate, significant* | Slang: *a big deal, cool with, stuff* |
| Polite requests: *Could you please…* (direct instructions are fine in clinical orders and procedures) | Blunt demands: *Send me…* |
| Usually no contractions in very formal writing | Contractions are fine |
| First person (*I*) is fine in letters and emails; reports usually use third person. No emojis | Any person, emojis |

**Ask:** Who will read this? Why am I writing? What does the reader already know?

**Purpose:** most writing aims to **inform** (explain facts), **persuade** (convince the reader), or **entertain**. A lab report informs; a scholarship essay persuades.

**Connotation:** words with the same dictionary meaning (**denotation**) can carry different feelings. *Thrifty* sounds positive; *cheap* sounds negative. In professional writing, usually choose words with a neutral connotation.

> **Check 1:** Rewrite for an email to an instructor: "Hey, gonna miss class, can u send the slides?"
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Plain language vs technical terms

- **With patients and the public:** use plain words and explain terms. *"We'll put a small tube in a vein in your arm to give you fluids."*
- **With other health professionals:** precise technical terms save time and avoid confusion. *"Start a peripheral IV and infuse normal saline at 100 mL/hr."*
- The right choice depends on the reader, not on which sounds smarter.

---

### 3. Tone and bias

- Keep tone **respectful and objective** in professional writing: avoid sarcasm, exaggeration, and emotional wording.
- Use **person-first** or preferred language: *a patient with diabetes*, not *a diabetic* in most clinical writing.
- Avoid unnecessary references to gender, age, or other traits.

> **Check 2:** Which is better in a lab report: "The results were super weird" or "The results were unexpected"?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Assuming formal means complicated.** Formal writing is clear and precise, not wordy.
- **Using jargon with a general audience.**
- **Slang hiding in essays:** *kind of, a lot of stuff, gonna, totally.*

---

### Learn it properly

- **Grammar, punctuation, and style handbook:** OpenStax *Writing Guide with Handbook*, Handbook: https://openstax.org/books/writing-guide/pages/handbook

---

### Check answers

1. Example: **"I will miss class on Thursday. Could you please send me the slides?"**
2. **"The results were unexpected."** Precise and objective.

## Building a Well-Organized Paragraph

**TEAS objective E.2.3** · Knowledge of Language

**What the TEAS expects:** you can pick a strong topic sentence, put sentences in a logical order, choose transitions, and spot sentences that don't belong.

---

### 1. Parts of a paragraph

1. **Topic sentence:** states the main idea, usually first.
2. **Supporting sentences:** details, examples, evidence, and explanation.
3. **Concluding sentence:** wraps up or leads to the next paragraph.

A good paragraph has **unity** (every sentence supports the main idea) and **coherence** (sentences flow in a logical order with clear transitions).

> **Check 1:** Which is the stronger topic sentence for a paragraph on sleep and studying: "I like to sleep in on Saturdays" or "Getting enough sleep improves memory and test performance"?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Ways to order ideas

| Order | Use it for | Signal words |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronological / process** | Steps, events, procedures | first, next, then, finally |
| **Order of importance** | Arguments, reasons | most important, above all |
| **Spatial** | Describing a place or object | above, next to, on the left |
| **Cause and effect** | Explaining why something happens | because, as a result, therefore |
| **Compare and contrast** | Showing similarities and differences | similarly, however, in contrast |

---

### 3. Transitions

| Purpose | Transitions |
|---|---|
| Add | also, in addition, furthermore |
| Contrast | however, on the other hand, in contrast, nevertheless |
| Cause / result | therefore, as a result, consequently |
| Example | for example, for instance |
| Sequence | first, next, then, finally |
| Conclude | in conclusion, overall |

**Pick the transition by the relationship** between the two sentences, not by how it sounds.

**Punctuation:** a transition that starts a sentence is followed by a comma (*However, practice helps.*). To join two complete sentences with one, use a semicolon before it and a comma after (*Math worries many students; however, practice helps.*). **"Although" is different:** it starts a dependent clause, so it never stands alone with a comma ("Although, practice helps" ✗). Use it like *because*: *Although math worries many students, practice helps.*

> **Check 2:** Fill the blank: "Many students fear the math section. ___, regular practice builds confidence." (Therefore / However / For example)
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 4. Finding the sentence that doesn't belong

Ask of each sentence: does it **support the topic sentence**? A sentence can be true and interesting and still be off topic.

---

### Common traps

- **Choosing a detail or example as the topic sentence.**
- **Choosing a transition that signals the wrong relationship.**
- **Keeping an interesting but off-topic sentence.**

---

### Learn it properly

- **Grammar, punctuation, and style handbook:** OpenStax *Writing Guide with Handbook*, Handbook: https://openstax.org/books/writing-guide/pages/handbook

---

### Check answers

1. **"Getting enough sleep improves memory and test performance."** It states a main idea the paragraph can support.
2. **However.** The second sentence contrasts with the first.

## The Writing Process

**TEAS objective E.3.1** · Using Language and Vocabulary to Express Ideas in Writing

**What the TEAS expects:** you know what happens at each stage of the writing process and can recognize a strong thesis statement and supporting evidence.

---

### 1. The stages

| Stage | What the writer does |
|---|---|
| **Prewriting (planning)** | Understand the task and audience; brainstorm, freewrite, or list ideas; narrow the topic; write a working thesis; outline |
| **Drafting** | Write a full first version, focusing on getting ideas down |
| **Revising** | Improve the **big picture**: ideas, organization, evidence, clarity; add, cut, move, or rewrite whole parts |
| **Editing and proofreading** | Fix **surface errors**: grammar, spelling, punctuation, capitalization, formatting |
| **Publishing** | Share or submit the final version |

The process loops: revising may send you back to prewriting.

**Memory hook:** **Re**vising = **re**-seeing the whole piece; **ed**iting = **ed**ging out small errors.

> **Check 1:** A writer moves a paragraph and adds an example. Is that revising or editing?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Thesis statements

A **thesis** states the main claim of an essay in one or two sentences. It is:
- **Arguable:** someone could disagree (not just a fact). Claims with **should** or **would** are usually arguable.
- **Specific:** focused enough to support in the essay.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| The TEAS has four sections. (fact) | Free TEAS preparation would help more students enter health programs. |
| Exercise is good. (vague) | Our town should add sidewalks along Main Street so older residents can walk safely every day. |

---

### 3. Supporting evidence

Strong support includes **facts, data, examples, expert sources, and reasoning** that connect directly to the thesis. Personal opinions alone are weak support.

**Citing sources:** when you use someone else's words or ideas, give credit. Copying without credit is plagiarism.

> **Check 2:** Which is a thesis: "Nursing is a job" or "Community colleges should expand evening nursing classes for working adults"?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Mixing up revising and editing.** Content and organization = revising; errors = editing.

## Word Parts: Prefixes, Roots, and Suffixes

**TEAS objective E.3.2** · Using Language and Vocabulary to Express Ideas in Writing

**What the TEAS expects:** you can figure out what an unfamiliar word means by breaking it into its prefix, root, and suffix. Many TEAS examples come from medical vocabulary.

---

### 1. How word parts work

**prefix + root + suffix**: *hypo* (below) + *glyc* (sugar) + *emia* (blood condition) = **hypoglycemia**, low blood sugar.

A **prefix** comes at the beginning, the **root** carries the core meaning, and a **suffix** comes at the end. Read the **suffix first**, then the prefix, then the root: *gastritis* = inflammation (-itis) of the stomach (gastr).

**About the "/o":** roots are often written with a slash and an *o* (cardi/o). The *o* is a connecting vowel, used when the next part starts with a consonant (*cardiology*) and dropped before a vowel (*carditis*). It stays between two roots, even before a vowel: *gastr**o**enterology*.

### 5. Everyday word parts

Non-medical words work the same way:
- **Prefixes:** *un*happy, *dis*agree, *in*correct, *im*possible (all mean not); *re*write (again); *mis*spell (wrongly); *bi*cycle (two); *poly*gon (many).
- **Roots:** *port* = carry (transport, portable); *spect* = look (inspect, spectator); *bio* = life (biology).
- **Suffixes:** read*able* (able to be); care*less* (without); kind*ness* (state of).

---

### Common traps

- **Confusing hyper- and hypo-.** Hyper = over; hypo = under.
- **Confusing -ectomy, -otomy, and -ostomy.** Removal, cutting into, making an opening.
- **Ignoring the suffix,** which often carries the key meaning.
