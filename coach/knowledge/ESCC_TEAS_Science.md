## Body Orientation: Directions, Planes and Cavities

**TEAS objective S.1.1** · Human Anatomy & Physiology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can describe where one body part is relative to another, name the plane that produces a given section, and say which cavity holds a given organ.

---

### 1. Everything starts from anatomical position

Every direction word in anatomy assumes the body is in **anatomical position**: standing upright, facing forward, arms at the sides, palms facing forward, feet flat and slightly apart.

**Why it matters:** "the thumb is lateral to the pinky" is only true with palms forward. If you picture the palms facing your thighs, the answer changes. On the TEAS, always picture anatomical position before you answer, even if the question shows a patient lying down.

> **Check 1:** In anatomical position, is the radius (thumb side of the forearm) medial or lateral to the ulna?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Directional terms come in opposite pairs

Learn them as pairs. If you know one, you know its opposite.

| Pair | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Superior / Inferior** | Toward the head / toward the feet | The nose is superior to the mouth. |
| **Anterior (ventral) / Posterior (dorsal)** | Front / back | The sternum is anterior to the heart. |
| **Medial / Lateral** | Toward the midline / away from the midline | The nose is medial to the eyes. |
| **Proximal / Distal** | Closer to / farther from where a limb attaches to the trunk | The elbow is proximal to the wrist. |
| **Superficial / Deep** | Toward the body surface / away from the surface | Skin is superficial to muscle. |

**The pair students mix up most:** proximal/distal versus superior/inferior. For the **limbs**, proximal/distal is the precise choice and the one TEAS questions usually expect. (Superior/inferior is still true in anatomical position, since the knee *is* above the ankle, but it is less specific.) For the trunk and head, use superior/inferior.

**Two body positions you may see:** *supine* means lying face up; *prone* means lying face down. Directional terms still refer to anatomical position, whichever way the patient is lying.

> **Check 2:** Which two words both correctly describe the knee compared with the ankle?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 3. Planes are the imaginary cuts

A **plane** is a flat surface that slices through the body. Imaging scans (CT, MRI) show the body in these planes.

| Plane | What it separates | Picture it |
|---|---|---|
| **Sagittal** | Left from right | A cut straight down the body from front to back. |
| **Midsagittal (median)** | Left and right into **equal** halves | A sagittal cut exactly through the midline. |
| **Frontal (coronal)** | Front (anterior) from back (posterior) | A cut from ear to ear, top to bottom. |
| **Transverse (horizontal)** | Top (superior) from bottom (inferior) | A cut across the waist, like a belt line. |

**Memory aid:** a *coronal* plane runs where a headband or a pair of headphones sits, up over the top of the head from ear to ear.

> **Check 3:** A scan slices straight across the abdomen at the level of the navel, separating the upper body from the lower body. Which plane is it?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 4. Body cavities hold the organs

There are two main cavities.

**Dorsal (posterior) cavity**
- **Cranial cavity:** brain
- **Vertebral (spinal) cavity:** spinal cord

**Ventral (anterior) cavity**, divided by the **diaphragm**:
- **Thoracic cavity** (above the diaphragm): the lungs, each in its own pleural cavity, and the **mediastinum**, the central region between the lungs. The mediastinum holds the heart (inside its pericardial cavity), the trachea, the esophagus and the great vessels.
- **Abdominopelvic cavity** (below the diaphragm):
  - *Abdominal portion:* stomach, liver, gallbladder, spleen, pancreas, most of the intestines
  - *Pelvic portion:* urinary bladder, internal reproductive organs, end of the large intestine (rectum)

**Why the diaphragm matters for questions:** it is the dividing line. If an organ is above it, the answer is thoracic; below it, abdominopelvic.

**Watch the ribs:** the liver, stomach and spleen sit tucked up under the lower ribs, but they are *below the diaphragm*, so they are abdominal, not thoracic.

**Kidneys:** they sit against the posterior abdominal wall, behind the lining of the abdominal cavity (retroperitoneal). If a TEAS question asks which major cavity, the answer is the abdominopelvic cavity.

**Serous membranes** line the ventral cavities with two layers: the **parietal** layer lines the cavity wall, and the **visceral** layer covers the organ. A thin film of fluid between them lets organs move without friction. Their names follow the cavity: *pleura* (lungs), *pericardium* (heart), *peritoneum* (abdomen). The heart's serous pericardium also sits inside a tough fibrous outer sac, and its visceral layer is the epicardium, the outer layer of the heart wall.

> **Check 4:** Name the cavity that holds the urinary bladder.
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 5. Quadrants and regions of the abdomen

Clinicians divide the abdomen to describe where pain or a finding is.

**Four quadrants** (two lines crossing at the navel). "Right" and "left" are the *patient's* right and left.

| Quadrant | Main organs |
|---|---|
| **Right upper (RUQ)** | Most of the liver, gallbladder |
| **Left upper (LUQ)** | Stomach, spleen |
| **Right lower (RLQ)** | Appendix, beginning of the large intestine (cecum) |
| **Left lower (LLQ)** | End of the large intestine (sigmoid colon) |

**Nine regions** (a finer grid): the middle column from top to bottom is **epigastric, umbilical, hypogastric (pubic)**. The sides are **hypochondriac** (upper), **lumbar** (middle) and **iliac or inguinal** (lower), on the right and left.

**Why it matters:** classic appendicitis pain settles in the right lower quadrant, and gallbladder pain in the right upper quadrant.

> **Check 5:** A patient points to pain in the lower right abdomen. Which quadrant, and which small organ is the usual suspect?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### Common traps

- **Answering from the patient's position instead of anatomical position.** Lying down does not change "superior."
- **Missing the more precise term:** for limbs, the expected answer is usually proximal/distal.
- **Reading right and left from your own side.** Right and left always mean the patient's right and left.
- **Putting the liver or stomach in the thoracic cavity** because they sit under the ribs.
- **Confusing sagittal with frontal.** Sagittal separates *left/right*; frontal separates *front/back*.
- **Putting the heart in the abdominal cavity.** The heart is thoracic.

---

### Learn it properly

If anything here didn't make sense, read the full explanation with diagrams:
**OpenStax *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*, Section 1.6 Anatomical Terminology:** https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/1-6-anatomical-terminology

---

### Check answers

1. **Lateral.** In anatomical position the thumb points away from the body, so the radius is lateral to the ulna.
2. **Proximal and superior.** The knee is closer to the hip (proximal) and higher than the ankle (superior). Proximal is the more precise term for limbs and the one TEAS questions usually want.
3. **Transverse.** A horizontal slice separates superior from inferior.
4. **Pelvic cavity,** the lower part of the abdominopelvic cavity.
5. **Right lower quadrant (RLQ);** the appendix.

## The Respiratory System

**TEAS objective S.1.2** · Human Anatomy & Physiology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can trace the path of air, explain how breathing moves air in and out, and explain how oxygen and carbon dioxide are exchanged.

---

### 1. The path of air

Air moves through a branching set of tubes that gets narrower and narrower:

1. **Nose (nasal cavity):** air is warmed, moistened and filtered by hairs and sticky mucus. (Air can also enter through the mouth, but the mouth does not filter it well.)
2. **Pharynx** (throat): shared with the digestive system.
3. **Larynx** (voice box): holds the vocal cords. The **epiglottis**, a flap at its top, covers the larynx when you swallow so food goes down the esophagus instead.
4. **Trachea** (windpipe): held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage.
5. **Bronchi**: the trachea splits into right and left main bronchi, one for each lung.
6. **Bronchioles**: smaller and smaller branches with no cartilage.
7. **Alveoli**: tiny air sacs where gas exchange happens.

The **right lung has 3 lobes; the left has 2** (making room for the heart). Each lung is wrapped in a two-layer **pleura** with a thin film of fluid between the layers. **Cilia** and **mucus** lining the airways trap particles and sweep them up toward the throat.

Everything from the nose to the **terminal bronchioles** is the **conducting zone**: it only moves air. The **respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts and alveoli** are the **respiratory zone**, where gas exchange happens.

> **Check 1:** Food goes down the esophagus; air goes down the trachea. Which structure keeps them apart during a swallow?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Gas exchange happens by diffusion

Each alveolus is wrapped in capillaries. The alveolar wall and the capillary wall are both extremely thin, so gases cross easily.

- **Oxygen** moves from the alveolar air (higher oxygen) into the blood (lower oxygen).
- **Carbon dioxide** moves from the blood (higher CO₂) into the alveolar air (lower CO₂), and you breathe it out.
- **In the body's tissues the directions reverse:** oxygen diffuses from the blood into the cells (which use it up), and CO₂ diffuses from the cells into the blood. Most CO₂ travels in the blood as **bicarbonate**.

**Why it works:** each gas moves on its own from where its concentration (partial pressure) is higher to where it is lower. No pumping is needed. In the blood, most oxygen rides on **hemoglobin** inside red blood cells.

**Surfactant**, made by cells in the alveoli, lowers surface tension so the alveoli don't collapse when you exhale.

> **Check 2:** In the tissues of your leg muscles, which way does oxygen diffuse, and why?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 3. How breathing moves air

Air flows from higher pressure to lower pressure. Breathing works by changing the size of the chest, which changes the pressure inside the lungs.

**Inhalation (inspiration)** is active:
- The **diaphragm** contracts and flattens (moves down), and the **external intercostal muscles** lift the ribs.
- The chest cavity gets bigger, so pressure in the lungs drops below the air pressure outside.
- Air flows in.

**Exhalation (expiration)** at rest is passive:
- The diaphragm and intercostals relax; the elastic lungs recoil.
- The chest gets smaller, so pressure in the lungs rises above outside pressure.
- Air flows out.

During hard breathing (exercise, coughing), exhalation becomes active: the **internal intercostals** and **abdominal muscles** contract to push air out.

**Bigger volume, lower pressure; smaller volume, higher pressure.** That inverse relationship (Boyle's law) is the whole mechanism.

---

### 4. What controls breathing

Breathing is controlled by respiratory centers in the **brainstem** (medulla oblongata and pons). The strongest everyday trigger to breathe is **rising carbon dioxide** in the blood (which also makes the blood more acidic), not falling oxygen. That is why holding your breath becomes urgent: CO₂ builds up.

> **Check 3:** You hold your breath. What change in your blood makes the urge to breathe so strong?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### Common traps

- **Putting the trachea before the larynx.** The larynx sits on top of the trachea, so air passes through it first.
- **Thinking exhalation at rest is muscular.** Normal exhalation is passive recoil.
- **Saying the diaphragm moves up during inhalation.** It contracts, flattens and moves down.
- **Thinking low oxygen is the main breathing trigger.** In healthy people it is rising CO₂.
- **Thinking gases are pumped.** Gas exchange is passive diffusion.

---

- **Airway structures:** OpenStax *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*, Section 22.1 Organs and Structures of the Respiratory System: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/22-1-organs-and-structures-of-the-respiratory-system
- **Breathing mechanics:** Section 22.3 The Process of Breathing: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/22-3-the-process-of-breathing
- **Gas exchange:** Section 22.4 Gas Exchange: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/22-4-gas-exchange

---

### Check answers

1. **The epiglottis.** It folds down over the opening of the larynx during swallowing.
2. **From the blood into the muscle cells.** The cells use oxygen constantly, so their oxygen level is lower than the blood's; oxygen diffuses down that gradient.
3. **Rising carbon dioxide** (and the acidity it causes). The brainstem responds by driving you to breathe.

## The Cardiovascular System

**TEAS objective S.1.3** · Human Anatomy & Physiology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can trace blood through the heart and body, name the chambers and valves, tell arteries from veins correctly, describe how the heartbeat starts, and name the parts of blood.

---

### 1. Two pumps, two circuits

The heart is two pumps side by side.

- The **right side** pumps oxygen-poor blood to the **lungs**. This is the **pulmonary circuit**.
- The **left side** pumps oxygen-rich blood to the **rest of the body**. This is the **systemic circuit**.

**Why the left ventricle is thicker:** it has to push blood through the whole body, which takes much more pressure than the short trip to the lungs. More work means more muscle.

**The heart's coverings and wall.** The heart sits in the mediastinum inside a sac, the **pericardium**. The wall has three layers: the thin outer **epicardium**, the thick muscular **myocardium** that does the pumping, and the smooth inner **endocardium** that lines the chambers.

**The heart feeds itself separately.** Blood passing through the chambers cannot nourish the thick heart muscle. The **coronary arteries**, the first branches off the aorta, supply the myocardium. A blocked coronary artery starves part of the muscle of oxygen; if the blockage lasts, that muscle dies. That is a heart attack (myocardial infarction).

> **Check 1:** Which ventricle has the thickest wall, and why?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. The path of blood through the heart

Learn this as one continuous loop. Each **atrium** receives blood; each **ventricle** pumps it out. Each valve sits at an exit and stops backflow.

1. **Superior and inferior venae cavae** bring oxygen-poor blood from the body
2. → **Right atrium**
3. → *tricuspid valve* → **Right ventricle**
4. → *pulmonary valve* → **Pulmonary trunk → pulmonary arteries** → **lungs** (blood picks up O₂, drops CO₂)
5. → **Pulmonary veins** bring oxygen-rich blood back
6. → **Left atrium**
7. → *mitral (bicuspid) valve* → **Left ventricle**
8. → *aortic valve* → **Aorta** → body

**Valve memory aid: "LAB RAT."** **L**eft **A**trium → **B**icuspid (mitral); **R**ight **A**trium → **T**ricuspid.

The **atrioventricular (AV) valves** (tricuspid, mitral) sit between atria and ventricles. The **semilunar valves** (pulmonary, aortic) sit where the ventricles empty into the big arteries.

**Heart sounds come from valves closing.** "Lub" (first sound) is the AV valves closing as the ventricles start to contract. "Dub" (second sound) is the semilunar valves closing as the ventricles relax.

> **Check 2:** Blood has just passed through the mitral valve. Where is it now, and is it oxygen-rich or oxygen-poor?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 3. Arteries and veins are named for direction, not oxygen

This is the most common TEAS trap in this system.

- **Arteries carry blood away from the heart.**
- **Veins carry blood toward the heart.**

Usually arteries carry oxygen-rich blood and veins carry oxygen-poor blood. The **pulmonary vessels are the main exception** (the umbilical vessels before birth are another):
- **Pulmonary arteries** carry *oxygen-poor* blood away from the heart to the lungs.
- **Pulmonary veins** carry *oxygen-rich* blood from the lungs to the heart.

The direction rule never breaks; the oxygen rule does.

**Vessel types in order:** arteries → arterioles → **capillaries** → venules → veins. Capillary walls are one cell thick, which is why capillaries are the main site where gases, nutrients and wastes are exchanged with tissues.

| | Arteries | Veins |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Away from heart | Toward heart |
| Pressure | High | Low |
| Wall | Thick, muscular, elastic | Thinner |
| Valves | No | Yes, many veins (especially in the limbs) have valves to stop backflow |

**Blood pressure** is written as two numbers, such as 120/80 mm Hg. The top number (**systolic**) is the pressure in the arteries while the ventricles contract; the bottom number (**diastolic**) is the pressure while they relax.

> **Check 3:** Why do veins in the legs need valves but arteries don't?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 4. What makes the heart beat

Heart muscle starts its own beat. The electrical signal follows a fixed path:

1. **SA (sinoatrial) node** in the right atrium fires first. It is the **natural pacemaker**.
2. The signal spreads across both atria, which contract.
3. **AV (atrioventricular) node** delays the signal briefly, so the atria finish emptying before the ventricles contract.
4. **Bundle of His (AV bundle)** → **bundle branches** → **Purkinje fibers** carry the signal through the ventricle walls, and the ventricles contract from the bottom up.

**Two terms to know:** *systole* = contraction; *diastole* = relaxation and filling.

> **Check 4:** Why does the AV node delay the signal?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 5. What blood is made of

- **Plasma** (about 55% of blood): mostly water, carrying proteins, nutrients, hormones, wastes and dissolved gases.
- **Formed elements** (about 45%):
  - **Red blood cells (erythrocytes):** carry oxygen on **hemoglobin**; no nucleus.
  - **White blood cells (leukocytes):** fight infection.
  - **Platelets (thrombocytes):** cell fragments that plug small breaks in vessel walls and help blood clot.

In adults, all three formed elements start in red bone marrow.

---

### Common traps

- **"Veins carry oxygen-poor blood."** Not always. Use direction: veins go *toward* the heart.
- **Mixing up tricuspid and mitral sides.** Tricuspid is right; mitral is left.
- **Thinking the atria pump blood to the lungs or body.** Only the ventricles do that.
- **Naming the AV node as the pacemaker.** The SA node sets the rate.

---

- **How the beat starts:** Section 19.2 Cardiac Muscle and Electrical Activity: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/19-2-cardiac-muscle-and-electrical-activity
- **Systole, diastole and heart sounds:** Section 19.3 Cardiac Cycle: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/19-3-cardiac-cycle

- **Blood:** Section 18.1 An Overview of Blood: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/18-1-an-overview-of-blood

---

### Check answers

1. **The left ventricle,** because it pumps blood through the entire systemic circuit, which needs much higher pressure than the pulmonary circuit.
2. **In the left ventricle,** and it is **oxygen-rich** (it just came from the lungs through the pulmonary veins and left atrium).
3. **Venous pressure is low,** so in the legs gravity could pull blood backward. When leg muscles contract they squeeze the veins (the skeletal muscle pump), and the valves make sure that squeeze pushes blood only toward the heart. Arterial blood is driven by high pressure from the heart.
4. **So the atria can finish emptying into the ventricles** before the ventricles contract.

## The Digestive System

**TEAS objective S.1.4** · Human Anatomy & Physiology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can put the organs of the digestive tract in order, say what each one does, tell mechanical from chemical digestion, and name the accessory organs and their jobs.

---

### 1. One long tube plus helpers

The **alimentary canal** (GI tract) is a continuous tube from mouth to anus. Food passes through these organs in order:

1. **Mouth:** chewing breaks food up; saliva starts digesting starch (salivary amylase).
2. **Pharynx:** swallowing.
3. **Esophagus:** a muscular tube that pushes food to the stomach by **peristalsis** (waves of muscle contraction).
4. **Stomach:** churns food and mixes it with acid and enzymes; begins protein digestion (pepsin). The acid (HCl) kills microbes and activates pepsin. The result is a soupy mix called **chyme**. A ring of muscle at each end controls flow: the **lower esophageal (cardiac) sphincter** keeps acid out of the esophagus, and the **pyloric sphincter** lets chyme into the duodenum a little at a time.
5. **Small intestine:** duodenum → jejunum → ileum. Finishes chemical digestion and absorbs most nutrients.
6. **Large intestine:** cecum (with the appendix attached) → ascending, transverse, descending and sigmoid colon → rectum. Absorbs water and electrolytes and forms feces.
7. **Anus:** elimination.

The **accessory organs** help digestion but are not part of the tube itself: the **teeth and tongue** in the mouth, plus the **salivary glands, liver, gallbladder and pancreas**. Food never passes through the glands, liver, gallbladder or pancreas; they send their secretions into the tract through ducts.

> **Check 1:** Put these in order: ileum, esophagus, duodenum, stomach, sigmoid colon.
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Mechanical versus chemical digestion

- **Mechanical digestion** breaks food into smaller pieces without changing the molecules: chewing, stomach churning, and segmentation in the small intestine.
- **Chemical digestion** uses enzymes to break the chemical bonds in large molecules.

| Nutrient | Broken down into | Where digestion starts | Key enzymes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrates | Simple sugars (such as glucose) | Mouth | Salivary amylase, pancreatic amylase |
| Proteins | Amino acids | Stomach | Pepsin (stomach), trypsin (pancreas) |
| Fats (lipids) | Fatty acids and monoglycerides | Small intestine (where almost all of it happens) | Lipase (pancreas) |

**Why the small intestine matters most:** pancreatic enzymes and bile arrive in the duodenum, and its lining finishes the job. Nearly every nutrient is both digested and absorbed there.

---

### 3. The accessory organs

- **Liver:** makes **bile**, processes absorbed nutrients, stores glycogen, and detoxifies drugs and alcohol.
- **Gallbladder:** stores and concentrates bile, and releases it into the duodenum when fatty food arrives.
- **Bile** **emulsifies** fat: it breaks large fat globules into tiny droplets so lipase can work faster. Bile contains no digestive enzymes.
- **Pancreas:** sends digestive enzymes (for carbohydrates, proteins and fats) plus **bicarbonate**, which neutralizes stomach acid, into the duodenum. (The pancreas also makes insulin and glucagon; see the endocrine page.)

> **Check 2:** A person has had their gallbladder removed. Which nutrient might they have more trouble digesting after a large meal, and why?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 4. Absorption

- **Small intestine:** its inner surface is folded, covered in finger-like **villi**, and the cells covering each villus have **microvilli**. This gives a huge surface area for absorbing nutrients. Sugars and amino acids enter blood capillaries; most fats enter lymph vessels (lacteals) first. Blood from the intestines goes first to the **liver** through the **hepatic portal vein**, which is why the liver processes absorbed nutrients.
- **Large intestine:** absorbs most of the remaining water and electrolytes. Its bacteria also make some vitamins, such as vitamin K.

---

### Common traps

- **Thinking the stomach absorbs most nutrients.** It absorbs little (alcohol and some drugs); the small intestine does most of it.
- **Thinking bile is an enzyme.** Bile emulsifies; lipase digests.
- **Thinking food passes through the liver or pancreas.** They send secretions into the tract through ducts.
- **Confusing peristalsis with chewing.** Peristalsis is the wave of smooth muscle contraction that moves food along.
- **Mixing up the colon order.** Ascending (up the right side), transverse (across), descending (down the left), sigmoid (S-shaped), then rectum.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Overview of the organs:** OpenStax *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*, Section 23.1 Overview of the Digestive System: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/23-1-overview-of-the-digestive-system
- **Small and large intestines:** Section 23.5 The Small and Large Intestines: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/23-5-the-small-and-large-intestines
- **Liver, pancreas and gallbladder:** Section 23.6 Accessory Organs in Digestion: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/23-6-accessory-organs-in-digestion-the-liver-pancreas-and-gallbladder

---

### Check answers

1. **Esophagus, stomach, duodenum, ileum, sigmoid colon.**
2. **Fat.** Without the gallbladder, bile trickles in continuously from the liver instead of arriving in a concentrated burst, so a large fatty meal is emulsified less well.

## The Nervous System

**TEAS objective S.1.5** · Human Anatomy & Physiology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can name the divisions of the nervous system, label the parts of a neuron and say which way signals travel, describe a reflex, compare sympathetic and parasympathetic effects, and match major brain regions to their jobs.

---

### 1. How the system is organized

- **Central nervous system (CNS):** brain and spinal cord. It processes information and makes decisions.
- **Peripheral nervous system (PNS):** all the nerves outside the CNS. It carries information in and out.
  - **Sensory (afferent)** neurons carry signals **in** to the CNS.
  - **Motor (efferent)** neurons carry signals **out** to muscles and glands.
    - **Somatic** motor division: voluntary control of skeletal muscle.
    - **Autonomic** motor division: involuntary control of heart, smooth muscle and glands. It has two branches, **sympathetic** and **parasympathetic**.

**Memory aid:** **A**fferent **A**rrives; **E**fferent **E**xits.

The brain and spinal cord are protected by bone, three membranes (the **meninges**) and **cerebrospinal fluid**. The spinal cord carries signals between the brain and body and handles simple reflexes.

---

### 2. The neuron

A neuron has three main parts, and signals move through them in one direction:

1. **Dendrites** receive signals from other cells.
2. The **cell body (soma)** holds the nucleus and integrates the signals.
3. The **axon** carries the signal away from the cell body to the **axon terminals**, which release **neurotransmitters** across a **synapse** to the next cell.

**Myelin**, a fatty wrapping around many axons, insulates them and makes signals travel much faster. Signals jump between the gaps in the myelin (nodes of Ranvier). Myelin is made by **Schwann cells** in the PNS and **oligodendrocytes** in the CNS. Neurons are supported by **glial cells** (neuroglia), which do not carry signals themselves.

The signal along the axon is an **action potential**: sodium (Na⁺) rushes into the cell (**depolarization**), then potassium (K⁺) flows out (**repolarization**). At the synapse the signal becomes chemical (neurotransmitter).

> **Check 1:** A drug blocks the release of neurotransmitter. At which part of the neuron does it act?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Reflexes

A **reflex** is a fast, automatic response that does not wait for conscious thought. A **reflex arc** has five parts: **receptor → sensory neuron → integration center (often an interneuron in the spinal cord) → motor neuron → effector** (muscle or gland).

---

### 4. Sympathetic versus parasympathetic

The two autonomic branches usually have opposite effects on the same organ.

| | Sympathetic ("fight or flight") | Parasympathetic ("rest and digest") |
|---|---|---|
| Heart rate | Increases | Decreases |
| Airways (bronchioles) | Dilate | Constrict |
| Pupils | Dilate | Constrict |
| Digestion | Slows | Speeds up |
| Blood glucose | Rises (liver releases glucose) | Favors storage (stimulates insulin release) |
| Main neurotransmitter at the target | Norepinephrine (mostly) | Acetylcholine |

**Why it makes sense:** in an emergency you want more oxygen and fuel to muscles and wider pupils to see; digestion can wait.

> *Answer at the bottom.*

### 5. Major brain regions

| Region | Main jobs |
|---|---|
| **Cerebrum** (cerebral cortex) | Conscious thought, memory, language, voluntary movement, interpreting sensations |
| **Cerebellum** | Balance, coordination, smooth and practiced movement |
| **Brainstem** (midbrain, pons, medulla oblongata) | Basic life functions: breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, swallowing |
| **Hypothalamus** | Body temperature, hunger and thirst, controls the pituitary gland |
| **Thalamus** | Relay station that routes sensory signals to the cortex |

The cerebrum's lobes: **frontal** (planning, personality, voluntary movement), **parietal** (touch and body sensations), **temporal** (hearing, memory), **occipital** (vision).

- **Reversing dendrites and axon.** Dendrites receive; the axon sends.
- **Thinking reflexes are processed in the brain.** Many simple reflexes are handled in the spinal cord.
- **Mixing up afferent and efferent.** Afferent = sensory, in; efferent = motor, out.
- **Mixing up cerebrum and cerebellum.** Cerebellum = balance and coordination.

## The Muscular System

**TEAS objective S.1.6** · Human Anatomy & Physiology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can compare the three types of muscle tissue, explain how a skeletal muscle contracts, and describe how muscles produce movement.

---

### 1. Three types of muscle tissue

| | Skeletal | Cardiac | Smooth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where | Attached to bones | Heart wall only | Walls of hollow organs and blood vessels (stomach, intestines, bladder, uterus, airways) |
| Control | **Voluntary** | Involuntary | Involuntary |
| Striations (stripes) | Yes | Yes | No |
| Cell shape | Long, cylindrical, many nuclei | Branched, usually one nucleus, joined by **intercalated discs** | Spindle-shaped, one nucleus |

**Why cardiac cells have intercalated discs:** they link the cells electrically and mechanically so the whole heart contracts as a unit.

> **Check 1:** Which muscle type is involuntary and striated?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. How a skeletal muscle is built

From large to small: **muscle → fascicles (bundles) → muscle fibers (cells) → myofibrils → sarcomeres.**

The **sarcomere** is the unit that contracts. It contains two kinds of protein filaments:
- **Thin filaments (actin)**, anchored to the **Z discs** at each end of the sarcomere.
- **Thick filaments (myosin)**, in the middle, with heads that can grab actin.

---

### 3. How contraction works: the sliding filament model

1. A **motor neuron** releases **acetylcholine** at the **neuromuscular junction**, which triggers an electrical signal in the muscle fiber.
2. The signal causes **calcium** to be released from the **sarcoplasmic reticulum** (the fiber's internal calcium store).
3. Calcium binds **troponin**, which moves **tropomyosin** out of the way and exposes binding sites on actin.
4. **Myosin heads** attach to actin (cross-bridges), pull the thin filaments toward the center, release, and reattach. Each cycle uses **ATP**.
5. The sarcomere shortens. The **filaments themselves do not get shorter**; they slide past each other, and the Z discs move closer together.
6. When the nerve signal stops, acetylcholine is broken down (by acetylcholinesterase), calcium is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, tropomyosin covers actin again, and the muscle relaxes.

**Why ATP matters:** it powers the myosin stroke and is needed to *release* myosin from actin. Without ATP after death, myosin stays stuck to actin; that is rigor mortis.

> **Check 2:** If calcium could not be released inside a muscle fiber, what would happen when the nerve fired?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 4. Producing movement

- Muscles **only pull**; they never push. So they work in pairs.
- The **agonist (prime mover)** does the movement; the **antagonist** does the opposite movement. Example: the biceps flexes (bends) the elbow; the triceps extends (straightens) it.
- **Tendons** attach muscle to bone. (**Ligaments** attach bone to bone.)
- The **origin** is the attachment that stays still; the **insertion** is the attachment on the bone that moves.
- Movement words: **flexion** (decreases the joint angle) versus **extension** (increases it); **abduction** (moves away from the midline) versus **adduction** (moves toward it); **rotation**.
- A **motor unit** is one motor neuron plus all the fibers it controls; recruiting more units produces more force. In **isotonic** contraction the muscle changes length and moves a load; in **isometric** contraction tension rises but length does not change (holding a plank).

**Other jobs of muscle:** maintaining posture, stabilizing joints, and generating heat (shivering).

---

### Common traps

- **Saying the filaments shorten.** They slide; their length stays the same.
- **Saying calcium binds myosin.** Calcium binds troponin.
- **Mixing up tendons and ligaments.** Tendon = muscle to bone; ligament = bone to bone.
- **Calling smooth muscle striated.** It has no stripes.

---

## The Reproductive System

**TEAS objective S.1.7** · Human Anatomy & Physiology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can trace the path of sperm and of the egg, say where fertilization and implantation happen, and match the main reproductive hormones to their effects.

---

### 1. Male reproductive system

**Path of sperm:**
1. **Testes:** sperm are made in the **seminiferous tubules**. Interstitial (Leydig) cells between the tubules make **testosterone**.
2. **Epididymis:** sperm mature and are stored.
3. **Vas (ductus) deferens:** carries sperm up from the scrotum into the pelvis.
4. **Ejaculatory duct**, then the **urethra**, which carries both urine and semen (not at the same time) out through the penis.

**Accessory glands** add fluid to make semen: the **seminal vesicles** (fructose for energy), the **prostate gland** and the **bulbourethral glands**.

**Why the testes sit in the scrotum:** sperm production needs a temperature a few degrees below core body temperature.

### 2. Female reproductive system

**Path of the egg:** ovary → released at **ovulation** → swept in by the **fimbriae** → **uterine tube** (where fertilization happens) → **uterus** (where the embryo implants).

- **Ovaries:** produce eggs (oocytes) and the hormones **estrogen** and **progesterone**.
- **Uterine (fallopian) tubes:** finger-like **fimbriae** sweep the released egg into the tube. **Fertilization normally happens here.**
- **Uterus:** a muscular organ where the embryo **implants** in the inner lining, the **endometrium**. Its thick muscle layer (myometrium) contracts during labor.
- **Cervix:** the narrow lower part of the uterus that opens into the vagina.
- **Vagina:** the birth canal; receives sperm.

---

### 3. Gametes and fertilization

- **Gametes** (sperm and egg) are made by **meiosis**, so each has half the usual number of chromosomes (23 in humans).
- At fertilization, sperm and egg combine to form a **zygote** with the full 46 chromosomes.
- The zygote divides as it travels down the uterine tube; about a week after fertilization the resulting ball of cells (a **blastocyst**) implants in the endometrium.

|---|---|---|

| **LH** (luteinizing hormone) | Anterior pituitary | A surge triggers **ovulation**; stimulates testosterone production |
| **Estrogen** | Ovaries (developing follicles) | Builds up the endometrium; female secondary sex characteristics |
| **Progesterone** | Ovaries (corpus luteum after ovulation) | Maintains the endometrium for pregnancy |
| **Testosterone** | Testes | Sperm production; male secondary sex characteristics |
| **Oxytocin** | Posterior pituitary (made in the hypothalamus) | Uterine contractions during labor; milk let-down |
| **hCG** (human chorionic gonadotropin) | The early embryo / placenta | Keeps the corpus luteum making progesterone in early pregnancy; it is what pregnancy tests detect |

In a typical 28-day cycle, ovulation happens around **day 14**. **GnRH** from the hypothalamus drives the pituitary to release FSH and LH.

**Why the period happens:** if no embryo implants, the corpus luteum breaks down, progesterone drops, and the endometrium is shed (menstruation).

## The Integumentary System

**TEAS objective S.1.8** · Human Anatomy & Physiology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can name the layers of the skin and what each contains, list the functions of the skin, and describe the accessory structures (hair, nails, glands).

---

### 1. Two skin layers, plus the layer beneath

1. **Epidermis** (top): **stratified squamous epithelium** (a stack of flat epithelial cells). It has **no blood vessels**; its cells get nutrients by diffusion from the dermis below. New cells are made in the deepest layer (**stratum basale**) and pushed upward. As they rise they fill with the tough protein **keratin** (these cells are called **keratinocytes**) and die, forming the protective outer layer (**stratum corneum**), which is constantly shed. **Melanocytes** in the basal layer make **melanin**, the pigment that protects against UV radiation.
2. **Dermis** (middle): tough connective tissue with **collagen** (strength) and **elastic fibers** (stretch). It contains blood vessels, nerves and touch receptors, hair follicles, and sweat and oil glands.
3. **Hypodermis** (subcutaneous layer, below the skin proper): mostly **fat** (adipose tissue). It insulates, cushions and anchors the skin to underlying muscle.

> **Check 1:** A paper cut stings but doesn't bleed. Which layer did it stay within?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. What the skin does

- **Protection:** a barrier against microbes, water loss, injury and UV light.
- **Temperature regulation:** when you are hot, dermal blood vessels **dilate** and sweat glands release sweat, which carries heat away as it evaporates. When you are cold, the vessels **constrict** to keep warm blood deeper.
- **Sensation:** receptors for touch, pressure, pain and temperature.
- **Vitamin D synthesis:** UV light starts the conversion of a cholesterol-based molecule in the skin into an inactive form of vitamin D; the liver and kidneys then activate it. Active vitamin D helps the intestine absorb calcium.
- **Excretion:** small amounts of wastes leave in sweat.

> **Check 2:** On a cold day your fingers look pale. What are the dermal blood vessels doing, and why?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 3. Accessory structures

- **Hair:** grows from a **follicle** in the dermis. The **arrector pili** muscle pulls the hair upright ("goosebumps").
- **Nails:** plates of hard keratin that protect the fingertips.
- **Sweat (sudoriferous) glands:**
  - **Eccrine** glands, all over the body, produce watery sweat for cooling.
  - **Apocrine** glands, in the armpits and groin, become active at puberty; bacteria breaking down their secretion cause body odor.
- **Sebaceous (oil) glands:** secrete **sebum** into hair follicles to soften skin and hair and slow water loss.

---

### Common traps

- **Thinking the epidermis has blood vessels.** It doesn't; that is why a shallow cut doesn't bleed.
- **Getting the layer count confused.** The skin itself is the epidermis plus the dermis. The hypodermis (subcutaneous layer) lies just beneath the skin and is often listed with them as a third layer. Know all three and what each contains.
- **Getting the temperature response backward.** Hot: vessels dilate and you sweat. Cold: vessels constrict.
- **Thinking melanin cools the skin.** Melanin protects against UV damage.

---

- **Layers of the skin:** OpenStax *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*, Section 5.1 Layers of the Skin: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/5-1-layers-of-the-skin

## The Endocrine System

**TEAS objective S.1.9** · Human Anatomy & Physiology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can match the main glands to their hormones and effects, and explain how negative feedback keeps hormone levels in range.

---

### 1. How hormones work

**Endocrine glands** release **hormones** directly into the blood. Hormones travel everywhere but act only on **target cells** that have the matching **receptor**. **Exocrine** glands (such as sweat and salivary glands) release their products through **ducts** instead. The pancreas is both: its islets are endocrine, and it also sends digestive enzymes through a duct.

**Compared with the nervous system:** nerve signals are fast and short-lived; hormone effects are slower to start but last longer.

---

### 2. The main glands and hormones

| Gland | Hormone | Main effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypothalamus** | Releasing hormones (such as TRH) | Controls the pituitary. It is the link between the nervous and endocrine systems. |
| **Anterior pituitary** | GH (growth hormone) | Growth of bone and muscle |
| | TSH | Stimulates the thyroid |
| | ACTH | Stimulates the adrenal cortex |
| | FSH and LH | Act on ovaries and testes |
| | Prolactin | Milk production |
| **Posterior pituitary** (stores and releases hormones made by the hypothalamus) | ADH (antidiuretic hormone) | Kidneys reabsorb more water |
| | Oxytocin | Uterine contractions in labor; milk letdown |
| **Thyroid** | T3 and T4 (thyroid hormone) | Sets metabolic rate |
| | Calcitonin | Lowers blood calcium |
| **Parathyroid glands** | PTH (parathyroid hormone) | Raises blood calcium |
| **Adrenal cortex** | Cortisol | Stress response; raises blood glucose; suppresses inflammation and the immune response |
| | Aldosterone | Kidneys keep sodium (and water follows) |
| **Adrenal medulla** | Epinephrine and norepinephrine | Fight or flight |
| **Pancreas (islets)** | Insulin (beta cells) | Lowers blood glucose |
| | Glucagon (alpha cells) | Raises blood glucose |
| **Thymus** | Thymosin | Maturation of T cells (most active in childhood) |
| **Pineal gland** | Melatonin | Sleep–wake cycle |
| **Ovaries / testes** | Estrogen, progesterone / testosterone | Reproduction; secondary sex characteristics |

**Pairs that oppose each other:** insulin vs. glucagon (blood glucose); calcitonin vs. PTH (blood calcium).

**Too much or too little:** too little insulin action → **diabetes mellitus** (high blood glucose). Too little ADH → **diabetes insipidus** (large amounts of dilute urine). Too much thyroid hormone → **hyperthyroidism** (fast metabolism, weight loss, heat intolerance); too little → **hypothyroidism** (slow metabolism, weight gain, cold intolerance). Too much GH in childhood → **gigantism**.

> **Check 1:** Blood calcium is low. Which gland responds, and with which hormone?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Negative feedback

Most hormones are controlled by **negative feedback**: the effect of a hormone switches off its own release. Like a thermostat, it keeps a value within a normal range.

**Blood glucose example:** after a meal glucose rises, so the pancreas releases insulin; glucose falls back, so insulin release drops. Between meals glucose falls, so the pancreas releases glucagon; glucose rises back.

**Thyroid example:** the hypothalamus releases TRH, which tells the anterior pituitary to release TSH, which tells the thyroid to release T3 and T4. When thyroid hormone levels get high, they **inhibit** the hypothalamus and pituitary, so less TRH and TSH are released and thyroid output falls.

**Positive feedback** is rare. It amplifies a change until an event ends it. Example: during labor, stretching of the cervix triggers oxytocin release, which causes stronger contractions, which cause more stretching, until the baby is born.

> **Check 2:** Is the insulin response to a meal positive or negative feedback?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### Common traps

- **Mixing up insulin and glucagon.** Insulin lowers glucose; glucagon raises it.
- **Thinking ADH and oxytocin are made in the posterior pituitary.** They are made in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary.
- **Mixing up the adrenal layers.** Cortex: cortisol and aldosterone. Medulla: epinephrine.
- **Calling labor contractions negative feedback.** They are a classic positive feedback loop.

## The Urinary System

**TEAS objective S.1.10** · Human Anatomy & Physiology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can trace urine from the kidney to the outside, describe the three steps of urine formation in the nephron, and explain how hormones adjust water and salt balance.

---

### 1. The organs

- **Kidneys (2):** filter blood and make urine. They sit against the back wall of the abdomen (behind the peritoneum, called **retroperitoneal**), partly protected by the lower ribs.
- **Ureters (2):** muscular tubes that carry urine from each kidney to the bladder by peristalsis.
- **Urinary bladder:** stores urine.
- **Urethra (1):** carries urine from the bladder out of the body. It is much shorter in females, which is one reason urinary tract infections are more common in women.

**Path of urine:** nephron → collecting duct → minor and major calyces → **renal pelvis** → **ureter** → **bladder** → **urethra** → outside.

**Inside the kidney:** an outer **cortex**, an inner **medulla** made of cone-shaped **pyramids**, and the funnel-shaped **renal pelvis** that collects urine.

> **Check 1:** One structure connects each kidney to the bladder; another drains the bladder to the outside. Which is which?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. The nephron makes urine

Each kidney has about a million **nephrons**, its working units. Urine forms in three steps:

1. **Filtration** at the **glomerulus** (a ball of capillaries inside **Bowman's capsule**): blood pressure pushes water and small solutes (glucose, salts, urea) out of the blood into the nephron. Blood cells and most proteins are too big and stay in the blood.
2. **Reabsorption** along the tubule, which runs in order: **proximal convoluted tubule** (where most reabsorption happens) → **loop of Henle** (helps concentrate urine) → **distal convoluted tubule** → collecting duct: useful substances move back into the blood. Normally **all the glucose** and most of the water and sodium are reabsorbed.
3. **Secretion:** the tubule adds extra wastes and excess ions (such as hydrogen and potassium) from the blood into the urine.

What is left flows into a **collecting duct**, where the final amount of water reabsorbed is adjusted (by ADH), and leaves as urine.

**Why it matters clinically:** glucose in the urine usually means blood glucose is so high the tubules can't reabsorb it all (a sign of uncontrolled diabetes). Large amounts of **protein** in the urine suggest the glomerular filter is damaged. **Blood** in the urine can come from a damaged filter or from anywhere along the urinary tract (for example, a kidney stone or an infection).

---

### 3. Hormones adjust the urine

- **ADH (antidiuretic hormone)**, released from the posterior pituitary when you are dehydrated, makes the collecting ducts reabsorb more water. Result: less urine, more concentrated. Alcohol suppresses ADH, which is why it increases urine output.
- **Aldosterone**, from the adrenal cortex, makes the kidney reabsorb more sodium; water follows the sodium, raising blood volume and blood pressure.
- The kidneys also release **renin** when blood pressure is low (which starts the chain that leads to aldosterone) and, when blood oxygen is low, **erythropoietin (EPO)**, which tells the red bone marrow to make more red blood cells.

> **Check 2:** A runner is dehydrated after a long race. What happens to ADH and to her urine?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 4. Jobs of the kidneys beyond making urine

- Remove nitrogen wastes (urea, creatinine)
- Regulate water and electrolyte balance
- Help regulate blood pressure
- Help keep blood pH stable
- Stimulate red blood cell production (EPO)
- Activate vitamin D

---

### Common traps

- **Swapping ureter and urethra.** Ureters (two): kidney to bladder. Urethra (one): bladder to outside.
- **Thinking filtration is selective.** Filtration is based on size; the kidney chooses what to keep during reabsorption.
- **Getting ADH backward.** More ADH means *less* urine.
- **Forgetting the kidney's endocrine jobs** (renin, EPO, vitamin D activation).

---

- **The kidney:** Section 25.3 Gross Anatomy of the Kidney: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/25-3-gross-anatomy-of-the-kidney
- **How urine forms:** Section 25.5 Physiology of Urine Formation: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/25-5-physiology-of-urine-formation

## The Immune System

**TEAS objective S.1.11** · Human Anatomy & Physiology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can tell innate from adaptive immunity, name the main immune cells and what they do, and explain active versus passive immunity, including how vaccines work.

---

### 1. Two lines of defense

**Innate (nonspecific) immunity** works the same way against any invader and is ready before an infection starts or acts within minutes to hours. It does not need earlier exposure.
- **Barriers:** intact skin, mucous membranes, mucus, stomach acid, tears and saliva (which contain the enzyme lysozyme).
- **Phagocytes:** cells that engulf and digest invaders. **Neutrophils** arrive first; **macrophages** are long-lived, large eaters.
- Other white blood cells: **eosinophils** fight parasites; **basophils** (like mast cells) release histamine; **lymphocytes** are the B, T and NK cells; **monocytes** become macrophages. Neutrophils are the most common white blood cell.
- **Natural killer (NK) cells:** destroy virus-infected cells and some cancer cells.
- **Inflammation:** redness, heat, swelling and pain (sometimes with loss of function) at an injury site.
- **Fever:** a raised body temperature that slows many pathogens.
- **Complement** proteins and **interferons** (signals that help nearby cells resist viruses).

**Adaptive (specific) immunity** targets one particular **antigen** (a molecule the body recognizes as foreign). It takes days to develop the first time but leaves **memory**, so the next response is faster and stronger.
- **B cells** become **plasma cells** that make **antibodies**, which tag and neutralize pathogens in body fluids. This is called **humoral** immunity.
- **T cells**:
  - **Helper T cells (CD4)** coordinate the response by activating B cells and other T cells. HIV destroys these cells.
  - **Cytotoxic T cells (CD8)** kill infected body cells directly. This is called **cell-mediated** immunity.
- **Memory B and T cells** remain after an infection or vaccination.

---

### 2. Inflammation, step by step

1. Damaged tissue and **mast cells** release chemicals such as **histamine**.
2. Nearby blood vessels **dilate** and become **leakier**: more blood flow brings heat and redness; leaking fluid causes swelling; pressure and chemicals cause pain.
3. **Phagocytes** squeeze out of the vessels and attack the invaders.

> **Check 1:** Why does an infected cut become red and warm?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Active versus passive immunity

| | Active (your body makes the antibodies; long-lasting memory) | Passive (you receive ready-made antibodies; temporary, no memory) |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural** | Getting the disease and recovering | Antibodies passed to a baby across the placenta or in breast milk |
| **Artificial** | **Vaccination** | An injection of antibodies (antiserum or immune globulin) |

**How vaccines work:** a vaccine shows the immune system a harmless form or piece of a pathogen (or gives your cells instructions to make a harmless piece, as mRNA vaccines do). The body makes its own antibodies and **memory cells** without having to get sick, so a later real infection meets a fast, strong response.

Antibody classes worth knowing: **IgG**, the most common, is the only one that crosses the placenta. **IgA** is in breast milk, saliva and tears. **IgM** is made first in a new infection. **IgE** drives allergies.

> **Check 2:** A newborn is protected from measles for a few months by its mother's antibodies. Which type of immunity is this?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 4. The lymphatic organs

The immune cells live in and travel through the lymphatic system.
- **Red bone marrow:** makes all blood cells, including lymphocytes. B cells mature here.
- **Thymus:** T cells mature here ("T" for thymus).
- **Lymph nodes:** filter lymph; they swell during infection as lymphocytes multiply.
- **Spleen:** filters blood and removes old red blood cells.
- **Tonsils:** guard the throat.

---

### Common traps

- **Calling antibodies innate.** Antibodies belong to adaptive immunity.
- **Saying T cells make antibodies.** Plasma cells (from B cells) do.
- **Calling a vaccine passive immunity.** Vaccines produce active immunity; the body makes its own antibodies.
- **Thinking antibodies need a previous exposure.** The first exposure produces antibodies too; memory cells are what make the *second* response faster.

## The Skeletal System

**TEAS objective S.1.12** · Human Anatomy & Physiology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can list the functions of the skeleton, describe the parts of a long bone, name the bone cells and what they do, and classify bones and joints.

---

### 1. What the skeleton does

- **Support** and shape for the body
- **Protection** of organs (skull: brain; rib cage: heart and lungs)
- **Movement:** bones act as levers that muscles pull on
- **Mineral storage:** especially **calcium** and phosphate, released into the blood when needed
- **Blood cell formation (hematopoiesis)** in **red bone marrow**
- **Fat storage** in **yellow bone marrow**

The adult skeleton has **206 bones**, divided into the **axial skeleton** (skull, hyoid, vertebral column, ribs and sternum) and the **appendicular skeleton** (limbs plus the shoulder and pelvic girdles that attach them).

> **Check 1:** Which part of the skeleton, axial or appendicular, includes the pelvic girdle?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Anatomy of a long bone

- **Diaphysis:** the shaft. Its wall is thick **compact bone**; the hollow center is the **medullary cavity**, filled with yellow marrow in adults.
- **Epiphyses:** the two ends, made of **spongy bone** covered by a thin layer of compact bone. The spaces in spongy bone hold **red marrow**.
- **Articular cartilage:** smooth cartilage covering the ends where bones meet at a joint.
- **Epiphyseal plate (growth plate):** a layer of cartilage where a long bone grows in length during childhood and adolescence; in adults it becomes the epiphyseal line.
- **Endosteum:** a thin membrane lining the medullary cavity.
- **Periosteum:** a tough membrane covering the outside of the bone; it contains blood vessels and nerves and is where tendons and ligaments attach.

---

### 3. Bone cells and remodeling

Bone is living tissue that is constantly broken down and rebuilt.

- **Osteoblasts** **build** new bone matrix.
- **Osteoclasts** **break down** bone and release calcium into the blood.
- **Osteocytes** are mature bone cells trapped in the matrix; they maintain it.
- **Osteogenic cells** are stem cells that become osteoblasts.

**Memory aid:** osteo**B**lasts **B**uild; osteo**C**lasts **C**ut.

Hormones steer remodeling: **PTH** raises blood calcium by stimulating bone breakdown; **calcitonin** lowers blood calcium. Weight-bearing exercise strengthens bone; inactivity weakens it.

> **Check 2:** In osteoporosis, bone becomes thin and fragile. Which cells are outpacing which?
> *Answer at the bottom.*

---

### 4. Classifying bones

| Shape | Example |
|---|---|
| **Long** (longer than wide) | Femur, humerus, phalanges |
| **Short** (cube-like) | Carpals (wrist), tarsals (ankle) |
| **Flat** (thin, often curved) | Skull bones, sternum, ribs, scapula |
| **Irregular** | Vertebrae, some facial bones |
| **Sesamoid** (sits within a tendon) | Patella (kneecap) |

---

### 5. Joints

Joints can be classified by structure: **fibrous** (such as sutures), **cartilaginous** (such as intervertebral discs and the pubic symphysis) and **synovial**. They are also classified by how much they move:
- **Immovable (synarthrosis):** sutures of the skull.
- **Slightly movable (amphiarthrosis):** the joints between vertebrae (with intervertebral discs) and the pubic symphysis.
- **Freely movable (diarthrosis), the synovial joints:** most limb joints. They have a joint capsule filled with lubricating **synovial fluid**. Types include **hinge** (elbow, knee), **ball-and-socket** (shoulder, hip) and **pivot** (between the first two neck vertebrae, which lets you turn your head), **saddle** (base of the thumb), **condyloid** (wrist, between radius and carpals) and **gliding** (between carpals).

---

### Common traps

- **Mixing up osteoblasts and osteoclasts.** B builds, C cuts.
- **Thinking all marrow makes blood cells.** Yellow marrow stores fat; red marrow makes blood cells.
- **Classifying by size instead of shape.** The phalanges are small but are *long* bones.
- **Forgetting the skeleton's mineral role.** Bone is the body's calcium reserve.

---

## Cells: Structure, Function and Division

**TEAS objective S.2.1** · Biology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can name the main organelles and their jobs, tell plant cells from animal cells and prokaryotes from eukaryotes, put the levels of organization in order, and compare mitosis with meiosis.

---

### 1. Levels of organization

**Atom → molecule → organelle → cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism.**

The cell is the smallest unit that is alive. A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job; an organ is built from several tissue types.

---

### 2. Organelles and their jobs

| Organelle | Job | Memory hook |
|---|---|---|
| **Nucleus** | Holds the DNA; controls the cell | Control center |
| **Nucleolus** | Region in the nucleus where ribosome parts are made | |
| **Ribosomes** | Build proteins | Protein factories |
| **Rough ER** | Studded with ribosomes; makes proteins for membranes and for export | |
| **Smooth ER** | Makes lipids; detoxifies drugs (abundant in liver cells) | |
| **Golgi apparatus** | Modifies, sorts and packages proteins for shipping | Post office |
| **Mitochondria** | Aerobic (oxygen-using) cellular respiration; make most of the cell's **ATP**, the molecule cells use for energy | Power plant |
| **Lysosomes** | Digest worn-out parts and debris with enzymes | Recycling center |
| **Cell (plasma) membrane** | Controls what enters and leaves; selectively permeable | Gatekeeper |
| **Cytoskeleton** | Protein fibers that give shape and move things inside the cell | |
| **Centrioles** | Help organize the spindle that pulls chromosomes apart during division (animal cells) | |

**Path of an exported protein:** ribosome on rough ER → ER → Golgi → vesicle (a small membrane bubble) → cell membrane → out of the cell.

> **Check 1:** A cell makes large amounts of a protein hormone for export. Which two *membrane-bound* organelles would you expect to be especially abundant?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Plant cells vs animal cells

Plant cells have most of the organelles listed above **plus** three structures animal cells lack:
- **Cell wall** (made of cellulose) outside the membrane, for support.
- **Chloroplasts**, where photosynthesis happens.
- A **large central vacuole** that stores water and keeps the cell firm.

Plant cells **also have mitochondria.** Chloroplasts make sugar; mitochondria break it down for ATP.

Going the other way, animal cells have **lysosomes** and **centrioles**, which plant cells lack.

---

### 4. Prokaryotes vs eukaryotes

| | Prokaryotes (bacteria, archaea) | Eukaryotes (animals, plants, fungi, protists) |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | **No**; DNA sits in a region called the nucleoid | Yes |
| Membrane-bound organelles | No | Yes (mitochondria, ER, Golgi…) |
| Ribosomes | Yes (smaller) | Yes |
| Cell wall | Most have one | Plants and fungi have one; animal cells do not |
| Size | Usually much smaller | Larger |

**Memory hook:** *pro* = "before" a nucleus; *eu* = "true" nucleus. Every cell has a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.

---

### 5. Moving things across the cell membrane

The membrane is **selectively permeable**: some things cross freely, others need help.

- **Diffusion:** particles spread from **high to low** concentration. No energy needed (**passive**). Oxygen and carbon dioxide cross this way.
- **Facilitated diffusion:** still high to low and passive, but through a protein channel or carrier (glucose enters many cells this way).
- **Osmosis:** diffusion of **water** across a membrane, toward the side with **more dissolved solute**.
- **Active transport:** moving particles from **low to high** concentration, "uphill." Requires **ATP** and a protein pump (the sodium-potassium pump).

**What happens to a red blood cell in different solutions:**

| Solution outside the cell | Solute concentration vs the cell | Water moves | The cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Isotonic** | Equal | In and out equally | Stays the same |
| **Hypotonic** | Lower | **Into** the cell | Swells, may burst |
| **Hypertonic** | Higher | **Out of** the cell | Shrinks |

**Memory hook:** hyp**O**tonic makes the cell swell like an **O**.

---

### 6. The cell cycle and mitosis

DNA is packaged into **chromosomes**. Before a cell divides, it copies each chromosome; the two identical copies, still joined, are called **sister chromatids**.

Most of a cell's life is **interphase**: it grows (G1), copies its DNA (S phase), and prepares to divide (G2). Then it divides: **mitosis** (the nucleus divides) followed by **cytokinesis** (the cytoplasm splits).

**Stages of mitosis: PMAT**
1. **Prophase:** chromosomes condense and become visible; the nuclear envelope breaks down; the **spindle** (a framework of protein fibers) forms.
2. **Metaphase:** chromosomes line up in the **middle**.
3. **Anaphase:** sister chromatids are pulled **apart** to opposite ends.
4. **Telophase:** two new nuclei form.

Some textbooks add a stage called **prometaphase** between prophase and metaphase. For the TEAS, PMAT is enough.

> **Check 2:** In which stage of mitosis do the chromosomes line up across the center of the cell?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 7. Mitosis vs meiosis

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Growth, repair, replacing body cells | Making gametes (sperm and eggs) |
| Number of divisions | One | Two (meiosis I and II) |
| Daughter cells | **2**, identical to the parent | **4**, genetically different (in females, one egg plus small polar bodies) |
| Chromosome number (humans) | Stays **46** (diploid) | Halved to **23** (haploid) |
| Crossing over | No | Yes, in prophase I |

**Diploid** means two sets of chromosomes (46 in humans); **haploid** means one set (23).

**Why halving matters:** at fertilization, a 23-chromosome sperm and a 23-chromosome egg restore 46.

---

### Common traps

- **Saying plant cells lack mitochondria.** They have both mitochondria and chloroplasts.
- **Giving plant cells lysosomes or centrioles.** Those are animal-cell structures.
- **Getting osmosis backwards.** Water moves toward the side with more solute.
- **Putting organ before tissue.** Cells → tissues → organs.
- **Saying mitosis halves the chromosome number.** Only meiosis does.
- **Mixing up the ER and the Golgi.** The ER makes; the Golgi packages and ships.

---

## DNA, RNA and Protein Synthesis

**TEAS objective S.2.2** · Biology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can describe the structure of DNA, pair complementary bases, tell DNA from RNA, and explain how a gene's sequence becomes a protein through transcription and translation.

---

### 1. The structure of DNA

- DNA is built from **nucleotides**. Each nucleotide has three parts: a **sugar** (deoxyribose), a **phosphate**, and a **nitrogen base**.
- DNA and RNA are **nucleic acids**. Two strands of DNA twist into a **double helix**. Sugar and phosphate form the sides of the "ladder"; base pairs form the rungs, held together by hydrogen bonds.
- **Base-pairing rules in DNA:** **A pairs with T**, **G pairs with C**. Bases that pair are called **complementary**.
- A **gene** is a stretch of DNA with the instructions for one protein (or a functional RNA). Genes are how traits are inherited.
- **Size order:** chromosome (one long DNA molecule) > gene > codon (3 nucleotides) > nucleotide.

> **Check 1:** One DNA strand reads G-A-T-T-C. What is the complementary strand, lined up base by base?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. DNA vs RNA

| | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar | Deoxyribose | Ribose |
| Strands | Two (double helix) | Usually one |
| Bases | A, T, G, C | A, **U**, G, C (**uracil replaces thymine**) |
| Where | Mainly the nucleus | Made in the nucleus, works mainly in the cytoplasm |
| Job | Stores genetic information | Carries and uses it to build proteins |

**Three kinds of RNA:**
- **mRNA (messenger):** carries the gene's code from the nucleus to a ribosome.
- **tRNA (transfer):** brings the matching amino acid to the ribosome.
- **rRNA (ribosomal):** part of the ribosome itself.

---

### 3. From gene to protein

**Step 1: Transcription (in the nucleus).** An enzyme, RNA polymerase, reads one DNA strand (the **template strand**) and builds a **complementary** mRNA. Same pairing rules, except **A in DNA pairs with U in RNA**.

**Step 2: Translation (at a ribosome in the cytoplasm).** The ribosome reads the mRNA **three bases at a time**. Each three-base unit is a **codon**, and each codon specifies one amino acid. tRNA molecules carry amino acids; each has an **anticodon** that pairs with the codon. The ribosome links the amino acids into a chain, which folds into a protein.

- The **start codon** is **AUG** (it codes for methionine).
- **Stop codons** (UAA, UAG, UGA) end translation.
- The **order of bases** in the gene sets the **order of amino acids**, which sets the protein's **shape**, and shape determines its **function**.

**Memory hook:** transcription keeps the same "language" (nucleic acid → nucleic acid, like transcribing notes). Translation changes language (nucleic acid → protein).

> **Check 2:** A DNA template strand reads T-A-C. What mRNA codon is made from it?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 4. Mutations

A **mutation** is a change in the DNA sequence.
- A **substitution** swaps one base. It may change one amino acid, change nothing (several codons code for the same amino acid), or create an early stop codon.
- An **insertion or deletion** of one or two bases shifts how every later codon is read (a **frameshift**), usually wrecking the protein.

Because sequence determines shape and shape determines function, a changed protein may not work. Sickle cell disease comes from a single-base substitution in a hemoglobin gene.

---

### Common traps

- **Putting T in RNA.** RNA has uracil (U) instead.
- **Swapping the locations.** Transcription happens in the nucleus; translation happens at ribosomes.
- **Confusing replication and transcription.** Replication copies DNA into DNA before cell division; transcription copies a gene into mRNA.
- **Reading single bases as codons.** A codon is three bases.

---

### Learn it properly

- **DNA structure:** OpenStax *Concepts of Biology*, Section 9.1 The Structure of DNA: https://openstax.org/books/concepts-biology/pages/9-1-the-structure-of-dna
- **Transcription:** Section 9.3 Transcription: https://openstax.org/books/concepts-biology/pages/9-3-transcription
- **Translation:** Section 9.4 Translation: https://openstax.org/books/concepts-biology/pages/9-4-translation

---

### Check answers

1. **C-T-A-A-G.** G pairs with C, A with T, T with A.
2. **AUG.** T pairs with A, A pairs with U (RNA has no T), and C pairs with G. AUG is the start codon.

## Mendelian Genetics and Punnett Squares

**TEAS objective S.2.3** · Biology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can use genetics vocabulary correctly, set up a Punnett square, and predict genotype and phenotype ratios for a single trait.

---

### 1. Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Gene** | A section of DNA with the instructions for one protein; genes underlie traits | Gene for seed color |
| **Allele** | One version of a gene | Y (yellow) or y (green) |
| **Dominant** | Shows when at least one copy is present; capital letter | Y |
| **Recessive** | Shows only when two copies are present; lowercase letter | y |
| **Genotype** | The alleles an individual has | YY, Yy or yy |
| **Phenotype** | The trait you can observe | Yellow or green |
| **Homozygous** | Two identical alleles | YY (homozygous dominant), yy (homozygous recessive) |
| **Heterozygous** | Two different alleles | Yy (a "carrier" of the recessive allele) |

**Memory hook:** *geno*type = the *genes* (letters); *pheno*type = what *appears*.

---

### 2. Mendel's laws

- **Law of dominance:** in a heterozygote, the dominant allele hides the recessive one.
- **Law of segregation:** each parent has two alleles for a gene but passes only **one** to each offspring. The two alleles separate when gametes form (in meiosis).
- **Law of independent assortment:** alleles for different genes (on different chromosomes) are sorted into gametes independently of each other.

---

### 3. Setting up a Punnett square

1. Write each parent's genotype.
2. Put one parent's two alleles across the top and the other parent's down the side.
3. Fill each box with one allele from the top and one from the side.
4. Count the boxes. Each box is a 1-in-4 (25%) chance.

In the figure, **P** is the parent generation, **F1** their offspring, and **F2** the offspring of two F1 plants.

**The four crosses to know (dominant A, recessive a):**

| Cross | Genotypes of offspring | Phenotypes |
|---|---|---|
| AA × aa | All Aa | All dominant |
| Aa × Aa | 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa | **3 dominant : 1 recessive** |
| Aa × aa | 1 Aa : 1 aa | 1 dominant : 1 recessive |
| AA × Aa | 1 AA : 1 Aa | All dominant |

> **Check 1:** Brown eyes (B) are dominant over blue (b). A heterozygous brown-eyed parent and a blue-eyed parent have a child. What is the chance the child has blue eyes?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 4. Beyond simple dominance

- **Incomplete dominance:** the heterozygote is a **blend**. Red × white snapdragons give pink. Pink × pink gives 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.
- **Codominance:** **both** alleles show fully. ABO blood type: alleles A and B are codominant and O is recessive. Type A can be AA or AO; type B can be BB or BO; type AB is AB; type O is OO.
- **Sex-linked (X-linked) traits:** the gene is on the X chromosome. Males (XY) have only one X, so a single recessive allele shows. This is why color blindness and hemophilia are more common in males. Example: a carrier mother (XᴴXʰ) and an unaffected father (XᴴY) have a 1 in 2 chance that each son has hemophilia, and no daughter will have it (each daughter has a 1 in 2 chance of being a carrier).

> **Check 2:** A red-flowered plant crossed with a white-flowered plant produces only pink-flowered offspring. What pattern of inheritance is this?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Mixing up genotype and phenotype.** "Tt" is a genotype; "tall" is a phenotype.
- **Reading 3:1 as a genotype ratio.** For Aa × Aa, 3:1 is the phenotype ratio; the genotype ratio is 1:2:1.
- **Thinking ratios are guarantees.** Each child has the same odds; a family with four children won't necessarily show 3:1.
- **Confusing incomplete dominance and codominance.** Blend = incomplete; both visible = codominance.

---

- **Mendel's experiments:** OpenStax *Concepts of Biology*, Section 8.1 Mendel's Experiments: https://openstax.org/books/concepts-biology/pages/8-1-mendels-experiments

## Biological Macromolecules

**TEAS objective S.2.4** · Biology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can name the four classes of biological macromolecules, their building blocks (monomers), their main jobs and examples, and describe how enzymes work.

---

### 1. The big four

Large biological molecules are mostly **polymers**: long chains of repeating small units called **monomers**.

| Macromolecule | Building block (monomer) | Main jobs | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | **Monosaccharides** (simple sugars such as glucose) | Quick energy; structure | Glucose, starch (plant storage), glycogen (animal storage, in liver and muscle), cellulose (plant cell walls) |
| **Lipids** | Glycerol and **fatty acids** (for fats); lipids are not true polymers | Long-term energy storage, membranes, insulation, hormones | Triglycerides (fats and oils), phospholipids, steroids such as cholesterol and estrogen |
| **Proteins** | **Amino acids** (20 kinds), joined by peptide bonds | Enzymes, structure, transport, defense, signals | Enzymes, hemoglobin, antibodies, collagen, keratin, insulin |
| **Nucleic acids** | **Nucleotides** | Store and carry genetic information | DNA, RNA (ATP is a modified nucleotide that carries energy) |

**Carbohydrate terms:** a **monosaccharide** is one sugar unit (glucose, fructose); a **disaccharide** is two (sucrose = table sugar, lactose = milk sugar); a **polysaccharide** is many (starch, glycogen, cellulose).

**Fats:** **saturated** fats have no double bonds in their fatty acid chains and are solid at room temperature (butter); **unsaturated** fats have one or more double bonds and are liquid (olive oil).

**Elements:** carbohydrates and lipids are mostly C, H and O. Proteins add **nitrogen** (and often sulfur). Nucleic acids contain C, H, O, N and **phosphorus**.

> **Check 1:** A molecule is made of a long chain of amino acids. What class of macromolecule is it?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Building up and breaking down

- **Dehydration synthesis:** monomers are joined into a polymer and a **water molecule is removed** at each bond.
- **Hydrolysis:** a polymer is split into monomers by **adding water**. Digestion is hydrolysis.

**Memory hook:** *hydro-lysis* = water splits it.

---

### 3. Energy per gram

- Fats: about **9 Calories per gram**.
- Carbohydrates and proteins: about **4 Calories per gram**.

Fat stores more than twice the energy per gram, which is why the body stores long-term energy as fat. Carbohydrates are the quickest fuel.

---

### 4. Phospholipids and membranes

A phospholipid has a **hydrophilic** (water-loving) phosphate head and two **hydrophobic** (water-fearing) fatty acid tails. In water they form a **bilayer**: heads face the watery inside and outside of the cell, tails hide in the middle. This bilayer is the basic structure of every cell membrane.

---

### 5. Enzymes

- Most enzymes are **proteins**. Their names often end in **-ase** (lactase, amylase, lipase).
- They are **catalysts**: they speed up reactions by **lowering the activation energy** (the energy needed to get a reaction started), and they are **not used up**.
- The molecule an enzyme acts on is its **substrate**. It fits into the enzyme's **active site**, which has a specific shape.
- **Denaturation:** high temperature or the wrong pH changes the enzyme's shape, so the substrate no longer fits and the enzyme stops working. Each enzyme has an optimum: most human enzymes work best near 37 °C; pepsin in the stomach works best at an acidic pH near 2. **Cold** slows enzymes down but does not denature them; activity returns when they warm up.

> **Check 2:** A patient has a very high fever. Why might some of their enzymes stop working?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Matching the wrong monomer.** Amino acids build proteins; nucleotides build nucleic acids; monosaccharides build carbohydrates.
- **Saying enzymes are used up.** They are reused.
- **Thinking all lipids are fats.** Steroids and phospholipids are lipids too.
- **Mixing up glycogen and glucose.** Glucose is the monomer; glycogen is the animal storage polymer.

## Microorganisms and Disease

**TEAS objective S.2.5** · Biology

**What the TEAS expects:** you can compare the main kinds of disease-causing microorganisms (bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa and parasitic worms), name example diseases, and explain why each kind needs a different treatment.

---

### 1. The main kinds of pathogens

A **pathogen** is anything that causes disease.

| Type | What it is | Example diseases | Treated with |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bacteria** | Single-celled **prokaryotes**: no nucleus, have a cell wall and their own ribosomes | Strep throat, tuberculosis, urinary tract infections, *Salmonella* food poisoning | **Antibiotics** |
| **Viruses** | **Not cells**: DNA or RNA inside a protein coat (capsid); reproduce only inside a host cell | Common cold, flu, measles, HIV, COVID-19 | **Antivirals** for some; vaccines prevent many. Antibiotics do **not** work |
| **Fungi** | Eukaryotes (yeasts and molds) with cell walls of chitin | Athlete's foot, ringworm, yeast (*Candida*) infections | **Antifungals** |
| **Protozoa** | Single-celled eukaryotes | **Malaria** (spread by mosquitoes), *Giardia* | Antiparasitic drugs |
| **Helminths** | Parasitic worms (multicellular) | Tapeworm, hookworm, pinworm | Antiparasitic drugs |

**Size order:** viruses are much smaller than bacteria; bacteria are much smaller than human cells.

> **Check 1:** A patient has a viral infection and asks for antibiotics. Why won't antibiotics help?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. A closer look at bacteria

- **Antibiotics** work by attacking structures and processes bacteria have and human cells don't, such as the bacterial **cell wall** and bacterial **ribosomes**.
- **Shapes:** **cocci** (spheres), **bacilli** (rods), **spirilla** (spirals). *Strepto-* means chains; *staphylo-* means clusters.
- Many bacteria are **helpful**: gut bacteria help digest food and make vitamin K, and "good" bacteria crowd out harmful ones.
- **Antibiotic resistance:** when antibiotics are overused, used when they aren't needed (for example, for viral infections) or not taken as prescribed, bacteria that are less affected by the drug survive and multiply. This is natural selection in action. Patients should take antibiotics exactly as prescribed.

---

### 3. A closer look at viruses

A virus attaches to a specific host cell and gets its genetic material inside (some inject only their DNA or RNA; most viruses that infect humans enter the cell whole). It then hijacks the cell's ribosomes and enzymes to make new viruses. The new viruses leave the cell, often destroying it.

Because viruses use the host's own machinery, few drugs can stop them without harming the host's cells. That is why **prevention by vaccines** matters so much for viral disease.

---

### 4. How disease spreads

- **Direct contact:** touching, kissing, sexual contact.
- **Droplet / airborne:** coughs and sneezes (flu, tuberculosis).
- **Contaminated food and water:** *Salmonella*, *E. coli*, *Giardia*.
- **Vectors:** an animal carries the pathogen between hosts. Mosquitoes carry malaria; ticks carry Lyme disease.
- **Fomites:** contaminated objects such as doorknobs or needles.

**Handwashing** is the single most effective way to stop the spread of many infections.

> **Check 2:** Malaria is caused by which type of microorganism, and how does it spread?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Calling viruses cells or bacteria.** Viruses are not cells and have no ribosomes.
- **Using antibiotics for colds or flu.** They target bacteria only.
- **Assuming all microbes are harmful.** Many bacteria are essential to health.
- **Calling malaria a virus.** It is a protozoan, spread by mosquitoes.
- **Calling ringworm a worm.** It is a fungal skin infection.

---

## Atoms and the Periodic Table

**TEAS objective S.3.1** · Chemistry

**What the TEAS expects:** you can describe the parts of an atom, use atomic number and mass number, explain isotopes and ions, and use the periodic table's layout to predict how elements behave and bond.

---

### 1. The parts of an atom

| Particle | Charge | Where | Mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Proton** | **+1** | Nucleus | About 1 atomic mass unit (amu) |
| **Neutron** | **0** | Nucleus | About 1 amu |
| **Electron** | **−1** | Outside the nucleus, in energy levels (shells) | Almost none (about 1/1,800 amu) |

- **Atomic number** = number of **protons**. It defines the element: every atom with 6 protons is carbon.
- **Mass number** = **protons + neutrons**. Electrons are too light to count.
  - Turned around, this gives the neutrons: **neutrons = mass number − atomic number**.
- In a **neutral** atom, electrons = protons.

**Worked example: how many protons, neutrons, and electrons are in chlorine-35?**

The number after an element's name is its **mass number**, so chlorine-35 (also written Cl-35) has a mass number of 35. The periodic table gives chlorine's atomic number: 17.

1. **Protons** = atomic number = **17**.
2. **Electrons** = protons in a neutral atom = **17**.
3. **Neutrons:** the mass number counts protons + neutrons, so 17 + neutrons = 35. Subtract the protons: 35 − 17 = **18** neutrons.

Check that it adds up: 17 protons + 18 neutrons = 35, the mass number. The 17 electrons aren't part of that sum because they are too light to count.

> **Check 1:** An atom has atomic number 8 and mass number 17. How many neutrons does it have?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Isotopes and ions

- **Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with **different numbers of neutrons**, so different mass numbers. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have 6 protons; carbon-14 has 8 neutrons instead of 6.
- **Ions** are atoms that have **gained or lost electrons**, so they carry a charge.
  - Lose electrons → **positive** ion (**cation**), e.g. Na⁺.
  - Gain electrons → **negative** ion (**anion**), e.g. Cl⁻.
- The **atomic mass** shown on the periodic table (e.g. 35.45 for chlorine) is a weighted **average** of the element's isotopes, which is why it is not a whole number.

**Memory hook:** ca**t**ions are "**pawsitive**"; **an**ions are **n**egative.

---

### 3. Reading the periodic table

- Elements are arranged in order of **increasing atomic number**.
- **Groups** (vertical columns) contain elements with the **same number of valence electrons** (for groups 1–2, the group number; for groups 13–18, subtract 10: oxygen in group 16 has 6) (outer-shell electrons), so they **react similarly**.
- **Periods** (horizontal rows) tell you the number of **energy levels** (shells) holding electrons.
- **Metals** are on the left and center (shiny, conduct heat and electricity, tend to **lose** electrons). **Nonmetals** are on the upper right (tend to **gain** or share electrons). **Metalloids** lie along the staircase line between them.

**Families to know:**

| Group | Name | Valence electrons | Behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Alkali metals** (Li, Na, K) | 1 | Very reactive; lose 1 electron → +1 ions |
| 2 | **Alkaline earth metals** (Mg, Ca) | 2 | Reactive; form +2 ions |
| 17 | **Halogens** (F, Cl, Br, I) | 7 | Very reactive; gain 1 electron → −1 ions |
| 18 | **Noble gases** (He, Ne, Ar) | Full outer shell (8; helium 2) | **Unreactive** (inert) |

**Trends:** across a period from left to right, atoms get **smaller** and **electronegativity** (pull on shared electrons) **increases**. Going down a group, atoms get **larger** and electronegativity **decreases**. Fluorine is the most electronegative element.

---

### 4. Chemical bonds

Atoms bond to reach a stable, full outer shell (the **octet rule**: 8 valence electrons).

| Bond | How | Usually between | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ionic** | Electrons are **transferred**; opposite ions attract | A metal and a nonmetal | NaCl (table salt) |
| **Covalent** | Electrons are **shared** | Two nonmetals | H₂O, CO₂, CH₄ |
| **Polar covalent** | Shared **unequally** (one atom pulls harder) | Nonmetals with different electronegativity | The O–H bonds in water |
| **Hydrogen bond** | A weak attraction between molecules (not a true bond within a molecule) | The H of one polar molecule and an O, N or F of another | Between water molecules; holds DNA strands together |

> **Check 2:** Sodium (group 1) reacts with chlorine (group 17). What type of bond forms, and why?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Counting electrons in the mass number.** Mass number = protons + neutrons only.
- **Confusing isotopes and ions.** Isotopes differ in neutrons; ions differ in electrons.
- **Mixing up groups and periods.** Groups are columns (similar properties); periods are rows.
- **Thinking a positive ion gained protons.** It *lost electrons*; the proton count never changes in chemical reactions.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Atomic structure, isotopes and ions:** OpenStax *Chemistry 2e*, Section 2.3 Atomic Structure and Symbolism: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/2-3-atomic-structure-and-symbolism
- **The periodic table:** Section 2.5 The Periodic Table: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/2-5-the-periodic-table
- **Ionic and covalent bonds:** Section 7.1 Ionic Bonding: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/7-1-ionic-bonding and Section 7.2 Covalent Bonding: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/7-2-covalent-bonding

2. **An ionic bond.** Sodium, a metal with 1 valence electron, gives that electron to chlorine, a nonmetal with 7. The resulting Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions attract each other.

## States of Matter and Physical and Chemical Changes

**TEAS objective S.3.2** · Chemistry

**What the TEAS expects:** you can describe solids, liquids and gases at the particle level, name each change of state, and tell physical properties and changes from chemical ones.

---

### 1. The three common states

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Fixed | Takes the shape of its container | Fills its container |
| Volume | Fixed | Fixed | Expands to fill the container |
| Particles | Tightly packed; vibrate in place | Close together; slide past each other | Far apart; move fast and freely |
| Energy of particles | Lowest | Middle | Highest |
| Compressible? | No | Barely | Yes |

A fourth state, **plasma**, is an extremely hot, ionized gas (stars, lightning).

---

### 2. Changes of state

| Change | From → To | Energy |
|---|---|---|
| **Melting** | Solid → liquid | Absorbed |
| **Freezing** | Liquid → solid | Released |
| **Vaporization** (evaporation or boiling) | Liquid → gas | Absorbed |
| **Condensation** | Gas → liquid | Released |
| **Sublimation** | Solid → **gas** directly | Absorbed |
| **Deposition** | Gas → **solid** directly | Released |

- Examples: dry ice sublimes; frost forming on a window is deposition; water droplets on a cold glass are condensation.
- **Evaporation** happens at the surface at any temperature; **boiling** happens throughout the liquid at the boiling point.
- During a change of state, the **temperature stays constant** until the change is complete: the energy goes into breaking or forming attractions between particles, not into raising temperature. Ice water stays at 0 °C until all the ice melts.
- Water's normal **freezing/melting point** is **0 °C (32 °F)**; its normal **boiling point** is **100 °C (212 °F)** at sea level.

**Memory hook:** going toward gas *absorbs* energy (you have to add heat); going toward solid *releases* it.

> **Check 1:** Sweating cools the body. Which change of state is happening, and does it absorb or release heat?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Physical vs chemical

**Physical properties** can be observed without changing what the substance is: color, density, melting point, boiling point, hardness, solubility, conductivity.

**Chemical properties** describe how a substance reacts to form new substances: flammability, reactivity with acid or oxygen, tendency to rust.

| Physical change (same substance) | Chemical change (new substance) |
|---|---|
| Melting, freezing, boiling | Burning |
| Cutting, crushing, bending | Rusting |
| Dissolving sugar in water | Cooking or baking |
| Mixing sand and iron filings | Digesting food |
| Breaking glass | Milk souring, fruit rotting |

**Signs of a chemical change:** a gas forms (bubbles), a color change, a precipitate (solid) forms from two liquids, light or a temperature change is produced, or an odor appears. These signs suggest, but don't prove, a chemical change: boiling also makes bubbles.

---

### 4. Special properties of water

- **Cohesion:** water molecules stick to each other (hydrogen bonds), which causes **surface tension**.
- **Adhesion:** water sticks to other surfaces, which helps it climb narrow tubes (capillary action) and forms the meniscus.
- **High specific heat:** water absorbs a lot of heat with little temperature change, which keeps body temperature and climates stable.
- **Good solvent:** water is polar, so it dissolves many substances.

---

### 5. Density

**Density = mass ÷ volume** (usually g/mL or g/cm³).
- An object **less dense** than a liquid floats in it; one **more dense** sinks.
- Water's density is **1 g/mL**. Ice is **less dense** than liquid water (about 0.92 g/mL), which is why ice floats. That is unusual: most substances are denser as solids.

> **Check 2:** A 40-gram rock has a volume of 10 mL. What is its density, and will it float in water?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Calling a change of state chemical.** Melting, freezing and boiling are physical.
- **Thinking temperature rises during melting.** It stays constant until melting is complete.
- **Mixing up sublimation and deposition.** Sublimation goes *from* a solid; deposition goes *to* a solid.
- **Assuming bubbles always mean a chemical change.** Boiling water bubbles too.

---

- **States and classification of matter:** OpenStax *Chemistry 2e*, Section 1.2 Phases and Classification of Matter: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/1-2-phases-and-classification-of-matter
- **Physical and chemical properties:** Section 1.3 Physical and Chemical Properties: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/1-3-physical-and-chemical-properties

## Chemical Reactions and Equations

**TEAS objective S.3.3** · Chemistry

**What the TEAS expects:** you can read and balance a chemical equation, apply conservation of mass, and identify the main types of reactions.

---

### 1. Reading an equation

**2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O**

- **Reactants** are on the left; **products** on the right. The arrow means "yields."
- A **subscript** (the small 2 in H₂O) tells how many atoms of that element are in **one molecule**. It is part of the formula and can't be changed.
- A **coefficient** (the big 2 in 2H₂O) tells how many **molecules**. It multiplies everything in the formula.
- State symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water.

**Counting atoms:** 3H₂O contains 3 × 2 = **6** hydrogen atoms and 3 × 1 = **3** oxygen atoms.

---

### 2. Conservation of mass and balancing

Atoms are **rearranged**, never created or destroyed, in a chemical reaction. So the **same number of each kind of atom** must appear on both sides, and the total mass of products equals the total mass of reactants.

**To balance an equation, change only the coefficients.**

**Worked example:** __ CH₄ + __ O₂ → __ CO₂ + __ H₂O
1. Carbon: 1 on each side. ✓
2. Hydrogen: 4 on the left, so put **2** in front of H₂O (4 H). 
3. Oxygen: now 2 (in CO₂) + 2 (in 2H₂O) = 4 on the right, so put **2** in front of O₂.
4. **CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.** Check: C 1 = 1, H 4 = 4, O 4 = 4. ✓

> **Check 1:** Balance: __ N₂ + __ H₂ → __ NH₃
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Types of reactions

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthesis** (combination) | A + B → **AB** | 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl |
| **Decomposition** | **AB** → A + B | 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ |
| **Single replacement** | A + **B**C → AC + **B** | Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ |
| **Double replacement** | AB + CD → AD + CB (partners swap) | AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃ |
| **Combustion** | Fuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (+ energy) | CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O |
| **Neutralization** (a double replacement) | Acid + base → salt + water | HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O |

**Memory hook:** synthesis *builds* one product; decomposition *breaks* one reactant; single replacement has one lone element on each side; double replacement swaps partners like a square dance.

---

### 4. Energy in reactions

- **Exothermic** reactions **release** energy (heat or light) to the surroundings; the surroundings get warmer. Burning and cellular respiration are exothermic.
- **Endothermic** reactions and processes **absorb** energy; the surroundings get colder. Photosynthesis is endothermic, and so is the dissolving inside an instant cold pack.
- **Oxidation** is the **loss** of electrons; **reduction** is the **gain** of electrons. They always happen together (**redox**). Memory hook: **OIL RIG** (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain).

> **Check 2:** An instant cold pack gets cold when squeezed. Is the process inside exothermic or endothermic?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Changing subscripts to balance.** That changes the substance itself (H₂O to H₂O₂ is a different chemical).
- **Forgetting to multiply the coefficient through.** 2H₂O has 4 H, not 2.
- **Mixing up synthesis and decomposition.** Check which side has the single compound.
- **Thinking mass is lost when a gas escapes.** It isn't lost; it just leaves an open container.

---

---

## What Affects How Fast a Reaction Goes

**TEAS objective S.3.4** · Chemistry

**What the TEAS expects:** you can explain, using collision theory, how temperature, concentration, surface area and catalysts change the rate of a reaction.

---

### 1. Collision theory

For a reaction to happen, particles must **collide** with **enough energy** (at least the **activation energy**, the energy needed to get a reaction started) and in the **right orientation**. Anything that makes successful collisions **more frequent** or **more energetic** speeds up the reaction.

---

### 2. The factors

| Factor | Change | Effect on rate | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Higher | **Faster** | Particles move faster: more collisions, and more of them have enough energy |
| **Concentration** (or gas **pressure**) | Higher | **Faster** | More particles in the same space collide more often |
| **Surface area** | Larger (powder vs a lump) | **Faster** | More particles exposed to collide |
| **Catalyst** | Added | **Faster** | Lowers the activation energy; not used up |
| **Stirring or mixing** | More | Faster | Brings reactants into contact |
| **pH** (enzyme reactions) | Near the enzyme's optimum | Fastest at the optimum | Too acidic or too basic changes the enzyme's shape |
| **Nature of reactants** | | Varies | Some substances are simply more reactive (sodium vs gold) |

**Everyday examples:**
- Food keeps longer in a **refrigerator** (lower temperature slows spoilage reactions).
- **Crushed** sugar dissolves and burns faster than a sugar cube (more surface area).
- A wood **fire** burns faster when you blow air on it (more oxygen = higher concentration).
- **Enzymes** are the body's catalysts; they let reactions run fast at 37 °C.

**Exception for enzymes:** heat speeds up most reactions, but too much heat (or the wrong pH) **denatures** an enzyme: its shape changes and the enzyme-driven reaction **slows or stops**. That's why enzyme activity plotted against temperature rises to a peak and then falls.

> **Check 1:** A tablet is crushed before it is dropped into water. Why does it dissolve faster?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. More about catalysts

- A catalyst provides an easier pathway with a **lower activation energy**.
- It is **not consumed**, so a small amount can be reused many times.
- It **speeds up** the reaction but does **not** change how much product can be made in the end.
- **Inhibitors** do the opposite: they slow a reaction.

---

### 4. Equilibrium (the basic idea)

Many reactions are **reversible** (⇌). At **equilibrium**, the forward and reverse reactions happen at the **same rate**, so the amounts of reactants and products stop changing, even though both reactions continue.

**Le Chatelier's principle:** if you disturb a system at equilibrium, it shifts to **counteract** the change. Add more reactant → it makes more product. Remove product → it makes more product to replace it.

> **Check 2:** Does adding a catalyst increase the amount of product at equilibrium?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Saying a catalyst is used up.** It is recovered unchanged.
- **Saying a catalyst raises the temperature or adds energy.** It lowers the energy barrier.
- **Thinking lower temperature stops reactions.** It slows them.
- **Thinking reactions stop at equilibrium.** Both directions continue at equal rates.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Factors affecting rate:** OpenStax *Chemistry 2e*, Section 12.2 Factors Affecting Reaction Rates: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/12-2-factors-affecting-reaction-rates
- **Catalysis:** Section 12.7 Catalysis: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/12-7-catalysis

---

2. **No.** A catalyst makes the reaction reach equilibrium faster, but it does not change the final amounts.

## Solutions and Mixtures

**TEAS objective S.3.5** · Chemistry

**What the TEAS expects:** you can use solution vocabulary, explain what affects solubility, work simple concentration and dilution problems, and predict the direction of osmosis.

---

### 1. Vocabulary

- A **solution** is a **homogeneous mixture**: evenly mixed throughout, so every part has the same composition (salt water, air).
- The **solute** is what gets dissolved (salt). The **solvent** does the dissolving (water). Water is the "universal solvent."
- A **heterogeneous mixture** is not evenly mixed; you can often see the parts (salad, sand in water).
- **Suspension:** particles are large enough to settle out over time (muddy water). **Colloid:** particles are small enough to stay spread out but large enough to scatter light (milk).
- **Unsaturated:** can still dissolve more solute. **Saturated:** holding the maximum at that temperature. **Supersaturated:** holding more than the maximum (unstable; extra solute can suddenly crystallize).

---

### 2. What dissolves in what

**"Like dissolves like":** polar solvents dissolve polar and ionic solutes; nonpolar solvents dissolve nonpolar solutes. Salt and sugar dissolve in water; oil does not.

**Factors that affect solubility and dissolving speed:**

| Factor | Solids in a liquid | Gases in a liquid |
|---|---|---|
| Higher **temperature** | Usually **more** soluble | **Less** soluble (warm soda goes flat faster) |
| Higher **pressure** | Little effect | **More** soluble (soda is bottled under pressure) |

**Stirring** and **crushing** the solute make it dissolve **faster** but do not change how much can dissolve.

---

### 3. Concentration

- **Percent (w/v, weight/volume)** = grams of solute per **100 mL** of solution. A 0.9% saline solution has 0.9 g of NaCl per 100 mL.
- **Molarity (M)** = **moles of solute per liter of solution**. (A **mole** is a counting unit for particles, like a dozen but far larger: 6.02 × 10²³.) 1 M NaCl has 1 mole of NaCl in each liter.
- **Dilution:** adding solvent lowers concentration but **not** the amount of solute. Use **C₁V₁ = C₂V₂**.
  - C₁ and V₁ are the concentration and volume of the strong solution you start with; C₂ and V₂ are the concentration and volume of the weaker solution you want to end up with.

**Worked example: how much 10% solution do you need to make 200 mL of 2% solution?**

1. Sort the numbers. Start: C₁ = 10%, V₁ = unknown (that's the question). End: C₂ = 2%, V₂ = 200 mL.
2. Plug them in: 10 × V₁ = 2 × 200 = 400.
3. Divide both sides by 10: V₁ = 400 ÷ 10 = **40 mL**.

So measure 40 mL of the 10% solution and add water until the total is 200 mL. Check: 10% of 40 mL is 4 g of solute, and 2% of 200 mL is also 4 g. The solute stays the same; only the water changes.

> **Check 1:** How many grams of glucose are in 500 mL of a 5% (w/v) glucose solution?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 4. Osmosis and tonicity

**Osmosis** is the movement of **water** across a selectively permeable (semipermeable) membrane **toward the side with more solute**. Memory hook: water follows salt.

| Solution around a cell | Compared with the cell | Water moves | Red blood cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Isotonic** (0.9% saline) | Same solute concentration | No net movement | Normal |
| **Hypotonic** (pure water) | Lower | **Into** the cell | **Swells**, may burst (hemolysis) |
| **Hypertonic** (3% saline) | Higher | **Out of** the cell | **Shrinks** (crenates) |

This is why IV fluids for most patients are isotonic, such as 0.9% normal saline.

> **Check 2:** A red blood cell is placed in distilled water. What happens and why?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Swapping solute and solvent.** The solute is the thing dissolved.
- **Thinking stirring increases solubility.** It only speeds up dissolving.
- **Assuming heat helps gases dissolve.** Warmer liquids hold *less* gas.
- **Getting osmosis backwards.** Water moves toward higher solute concentration.

---

## Acids, Bases and pH

**TEAS objective S.3.6** · Chemistry

**What the TEAS expects:** you can describe acids and bases, read the pH scale (including the tenfold rule), predict the products of neutralization, and explain what a buffer does.

---

### 1. Acids and bases

| | Acids | Bases |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Release **H⁺** (hydrogen ions) in water; proton **donors** (an H⁺ ion is just a proton) | Release **OH⁻** (hydroxide ions) or accept H⁺; proton **acceptors** |
| pH | **Below 7** | **Above 7** |
| Taste and feel | Sour | Bitter, slippery |
| Litmus paper | Turns **red** | Turns **blue** |
| Examples | Stomach acid (HCl), vinegar, lemon juice, carbonic acid | Sodium hydroxide (lye), ammonia, baking soda, bleach |

**Strong vs weak:** a **strong** acid or base **ionizes completely** (breaks apart into ions) in water (HCl, NaOH). A **weak** one ionizes only partly (acetic acid in vinegar, ammonia). "Strong" is about how completely it ionizes, not how concentrated it is.

---

### 2. The pH scale

- pH runs from **0 to 14**. **7 is neutral** (pure water).
- **Lower pH = more H⁺ = more acidic.**
- Each pH unit is a **tenfold** change in H⁺ concentration. pH 3 is **10** times more acidic than pH 4 and **100** times more acidic than pH 5.
- **Normal blood pH is 7.35–7.45**, slightly basic. Below 7.35 is **acidosis**; above 7.45 is **alkalosis**. Stomach acid is about pH 1.5–3.5.

> **Check 1:** Solution X has pH 2 and solution Y has pH 5. How many times greater is the H⁺ concentration of X?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Neutralization

**Acid + base → salt + water.**

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

The H⁺ from the acid and the OH⁻ from the base combine to form water; the leftover ions form a salt. Antacids work this way: a weak base (such as calcium carbonate or magnesium hydroxide) neutralizes excess stomach acid.

---

### 4. Buffers

A **buffer** **resists changes in pH** when small amounts of acid or base are added. It is a mix of a weak acid and its matching base, so it can absorb extra H⁺ or extra OH⁻.

The main buffer in blood is the **carbonic acid–bicarbonate** system (H₂CO₃ / HCO₃⁻). CO₂ dissolved in blood forms carbonic acid, so the lungs (by exhaling CO₂, which raises pH) and kidneys (by adjusting bicarbonate) help keep blood pH in its narrow range.

> **Check 2:** What is the main job of a buffer?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Reading pH backwards.** A *lower* pH is *more* acidic.
- **Treating pH steps as equal amounts.** Each step is ×10, so two steps is ×100.
- **Confusing strong with concentrated.** Strong means fully ionized.
- **Calling blood neutral.** Normal blood is slightly basic (7.35–7.45).

---

### Learn it properly

- **Acids and bases:** OpenStax *Chemistry 2e*, Section 14.1 Brønsted-Lowry Acids and Bases: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/14-1-bronsted-lowry-acids-and-bases
- **pH:** Section 14.2 pH and pOH: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/14-2-ph-and-poh
- **Buffers, including blood:** Section 14.6 Buffers: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/14-6-buffers

---

1. **1,000 times.** The pH values differ by 3, and 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000.
2. **To resist changes in pH** when a small amount of acid or base is added.

## Scientific Measurement and Tools

**TEAS objective S.4.1** · Scientific Reasoning

**What the TEAS expects:** you can use metric units and prefixes, convert between them, pick the right tool for a measurement, read instruments correctly, and tell accuracy from precision.

---

### 1. Base units (metric / SI)

| Quantity | Base unit | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| Length | **meter (m)** | Ruler, meter stick |
| Mass | **gram (g)** (the SI base unit is the kilogram) | **Balance** (triple-beam or electronic) |
| Volume of a liquid | **liter (L)** | **Graduated cylinder**, pipette, volumetric flask (beakers and Erlenmeyer flasks have only rough markings; don't use them to measure) |
| Temperature | **degrees Celsius (°C)**; kelvin (K) in science | Thermometer |
| Time | **second (s)** | Stopwatch |

**Mass vs weight:** mass is the amount of matter (measured with a balance; the same on the Moon). Weight is the pull of gravity on that mass (measured with a scale; less on the Moon).

**Volume of a solid:** 1 mL = 1 cm³ (cubic centimeter, also written cc). For an irregular object, use **water displacement**: the rise in water level in a graduated cylinder equals the object's volume.

---

### 2. Metric prefixes

| Prefix | Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **kilo-** | k | 1,000 × base |
| hecto- | h | 100 × base |
| deka- | da | 10 × base |
| (base unit) | | 1 |
| **deci-** | d | 1/10 of base |
| **centi-** | c | 1/100 of base |
| **milli-** | m | 1/1,000 of base |
| **micro-** | μ (or mc in medicine) | 1/1,000,000 of base: **3 steps below milli**, so 1 mg = 1,000 mcg |

**Memory hook (largest to smallest):** **K**ing **H**enry **D**ied **B**y **D**rinking **C**hocolate **M**ilk = **k**ilo, **h**ecto, **d**eka, **B**ase, **d**eci, **c**enti, **m**illi. Each step from kilo down to milli is a factor of 10. Micro is a jump of 1,000 below milli, not 10.

**Converting:** moving to a **smaller** unit gives a **bigger** number (multiply); moving to a **larger** unit gives a **smaller** number (divide). Count the steps and move the decimal point that many places.

- 2.5 kg → g: 3 steps smaller → **2,500 g**
- 450 mg → g: 3 steps larger → **0.45 g**
- 35 mm → cm: 1 step larger → **3.5 cm**
- 0.5 mg → mcg: micro is 1,000 times smaller → **500 mcg**

> **Check 1:** Convert 0.75 liters to milliliters.
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Temperature

- Water freezes at **0 °C (32 °F)** and boils at **100 °C (212 °F)**. Normal body temperature is about **37 °C (98.6 °F)**.
- **°F = (°C × 9/5) + 32**  and  **°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9**
- **Worked example:** a fever of 104 °F → (104 − 32) × 5/9 = 72 × 5/9 = **40 °C**.
- **K = °C + 273** (0 K is absolute zero; there are no negative kelvin temperatures).

---

### 4. Reading instruments

- **Read a graduated cylinder at the bottom of the meniscus** (the curved water surface), with your **eye level** with it. Looking from above or below gives a wrong reading (parallax error).
- Read **one digit beyond** the smallest marking by estimating between lines. If a cylinder is marked every 1 mL and the meniscus is halfway between 36 and 37, record **36.5 mL**.
- **Zero (tare)** the balance with the empty container on it, so the reading shows only the sample. (If you don't tare, subtract the container's mass instead; never do both.)

---

### 5. Accuracy vs precision

- **Accuracy:** how close a measurement is to the **true** value.
- **Precision:** how close repeated measurements are to **each other**.

Think of a dartboard: darts tightly grouped but far from the bullseye are **precise but not accurate**. Darts scattered evenly around the bullseye are **accurate on average but not precise**. A miscalibrated scale that always reads 2 kg high gives precise but inaccurate results.

> **Check 2:** A student weighs the same 10.0 g sample three times and gets 12.1 g, 12.0 g and 12.1 g. Are the results accurate, precise, both, or neither?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Moving the decimal the wrong way.** Smaller unit → bigger number.
- **Confusing mass and weight.** A balance measures mass.
- **Reading the top of the meniscus.** Read the bottom, at eye level.
- **Treating precise as accurate.** Consistent results can all be wrong.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Units and prefixes:** OpenStax *Chemistry 2e*, Section 1.4 Measurements: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/1-4-measurements
- **Accuracy and precision:** Section 1.5 Measurement Uncertainty, Accuracy, and Precision: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/1-5-measurement-uncertainty-accuracy-and-precision
- **Unit conversions and temperature:** Section 1.6 Mathematical Treatment of Measurement Results: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/1-6-mathematical-treatment-of-measurement-results

---

### Check answers

1. **750 mL.** Liters to milliliters is 3 steps smaller, so multiply by 1,000.
2. **Precise but not accurate.** The readings agree closely with each other but are about 2 g above the true mass.

## Logic and Evidence in Science

**TEAS objective S.4.2** · Scientific Reasoning

**What the TEAS expects:** you can tell observations from inferences, judge which evidence best supports or weakens a claim, recognize that correlation does not prove causation, and choose the conclusion the data actually support.

---

### 1. Observation, inference, hypothesis, theory, law

| Term | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Observation** | What you directly see or measure | "The patient's temperature is 39 °C." |
| **Inference** | A conclusion drawn from observations | "The patient probably has an infection." |
| **Hypothesis** | A testable, falsifiable prediction (one that a result could prove wrong) | "If patients take drug X, their fever will drop faster than with a placebo." |
| **Theory** | A well-tested, broad **explanation** supported by much evidence | Cell theory, germ theory |
| **Law** | A description of **what** always happens, often as an equation; it does not explain why | Boyle's law, law of conservation of mass |

A theory does **not** become a law with more evidence; they do different jobs (explaining vs describing).

> **Check 1:** "The leaves are yellow" or "The plant is not getting enough nitrogen": which is the observation and which is the inference?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Judging evidence

Stronger evidence usually:
- comes from a **controlled experiment** (a control group, one variable changed),
- uses a **large, random** sample,
- is **measured**, not just reported or felt,
- has been **repeated** (replicated) by others,
- comes from a source **without a conflict of interest** and is **peer reviewed**.

Weaker evidence: a single **anecdote** or testimonial, a tiny or self-selected sample, an advertisement, an opinion, or a result no one has repeated.

**Best design for "does treatment X work?":** a **randomized controlled trial**, ideally **double-blind** (neither patients nor researchers know who got the treatment) with a **placebo** group (a group given a fake treatment, such as a sugar pill).

---

### 3. Inductive and deductive reasoning

- **Inductive:** from specific observations to a general rule. "Every swan I've seen is white, so all swans are white." Useful, but a new observation can overturn it.
- **Deductive:** from a general rule to a specific conclusion. "All mammals have hair; a whale is a mammal; so a whale has hair." If the premises are true, the conclusion must be true.

---

### 4. Correlation is not causation

A **correlation** means two things change together. It does not show that one **causes** the other.

- **Third (confounding) variable:** ice cream sales and drowning both rise in summer. Hot weather drives both.
- **Reverse causation:** people with more hospital visits are sicker, but the illness causes the visits, not the other way around.
- **Coincidence:** with enough data, some things line up by chance.

To show causation, change one variable on purpose in a controlled experiment and see whether the other changes.

---

### 5. Choosing the right conclusion

On the TEAS, the best conclusion:
- is **supported by the data given**, not by outside facts,
- does **not overreach** (a study of adults says nothing certain about children; one experiment does not "prove" a hypothesis),
- matches the **strength** of the evidence ("suggests" vs "proves").

Watch for answer choices with **always**, **never**, **proves** or **all**. They often claim more than the evidence shows.

**Supporting vs weakening:** evidence **supports** a claim if it is what you would expect if the claim were true. It **weakens** the claim if it shows the predicted effect doesn't happen, or points to another explanation.

> **Check 2:** A study finds that people who carry lighters are more likely to get lung cancer. A news headline says "Lighters cause cancer." What is the most likely explanation for the link?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Calling an inference an observation.** Words like "probably," "because" and "seems" signal inference.
- **Trusting testimonials.** One person's experience is weak evidence.
- **Jumping from correlation to cause.** Look for a third variable.
- **Picking the "proves" answer.** Experiments support or refute; they rarely prove.

---

### Learn it properly

- **How science works:** OpenStax *Concepts of Biology*, Section 1.2 The Process of Science: https://openstax.org/books/concepts-biology/pages/1-2-the-process-of-science

---

### Check answers

1. **"The leaves are yellow" is the observation**; "not getting enough nitrogen" is an inference that tries to explain it.
2. **A third variable: smoking.** Smokers are more likely to carry lighters and more likely to get lung cancer.

## Predicting Relationships from Data

**TEAS objective S.4.3** · Scientific Reasoning

**What the TEAS expects:** you can read a trend in a table or graph, recognize direct and inverse relationships, and use a pattern or a cause-and-effect chain to predict what will happen next.

---

### 1. Kinds of relationships

| Relationship | As X increases, Y… | Graph shape | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct (positive)** | Increases | Line slopes **up** | More exercise → higher heart rate |
| **Inverse (negative)** | Decreases | Line slopes **down** | Higher altitude → lower boiling point; larger gas volume → lower pressure |
| **No relationship** | Shows no pattern | Scattered points or a flat line | Shoe size and test scores |
| **Rises then falls** | Increases to a peak, then decreases | Hill-shaped | Enzyme activity vs temperature |
| **Levels off** | Increases, then stops changing | Curve that flattens | Enzyme reaction rate vs amount of substrate (levels off once all the enzyme is busy) |

On a graph, the **independent variable** (the one changed on purpose) goes on the **x-axis**; the **dependent variable** (the result) goes on the **y-axis**.

---

### 2. Making predictions from a pattern

1. Find the **step size** or rate of change. Example: boiling point drops 5 °C per 1,500 m of altitude.
2. Check whether the pattern is **steady** (same change each step) or **changing** (doubling, slowing, peaking).
3. **Interpolate** (predict between known points) with more confidence than you **extrapolate** (predict beyond them). Extrapolating assumes the pattern continues, which may not be true.

**Doubling pattern:** bacteria that double every 20 minutes go 100 → 200 → 400 → 800 in one hour. Not 100 → 200 → 300.

> **Check 1:** A plant grows 2 cm in week 1, 4 cm in week 2, and 6 cm in week 3 (each number is that week's growth). If the pattern continues, how much will it grow in week 4?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Cause-and-effect chains

Many TEAS questions ask you to follow a chain of events. Work one link at a time.

**Example:** a person becomes dehydrated → the blood becomes more concentrated and blood volume falls → the hypothalamus senses this, and ADH is released from the posterior pituitary → the kidneys reabsorb more water → urine becomes **less** in volume and **more** concentrated.

**Example:** more CO₂ in the blood → blood becomes more acidic (pH falls) → the brainstem increases the breathing rate → more CO₂ is exhaled → pH rises back toward normal.

**Food chains work the same way:** if a predator population falls, its prey population usually **rises**, and then the plants the prey eat **decrease**.

---

### 4. Reading graphs carefully

- Read the **title, axis labels and units** first.
- Check the **scale**: does each grid line equal 1, 5 or 10?
- Find the **trend** before looking at single points.
- Answer **only** from what the graph shows.

> **Check 2:** In a pond, frogs eat insects and snakes eat frogs. If a disease kills most of the snakes, what will most likely happen to the insect population at first?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Assuming every trend is a straight line.** Look for peaks, plateaus and doubling.
- **Swapping the axes.** Independent on x, dependent on y.
- **Extrapolating too far.** The pattern may change outside the data.
- **Skipping links in a chain.** Trace each step.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Variables and experimental design:** OpenStax *Concepts of Biology*, Section 1.2 The Process of Science: https://openstax.org/books/concepts-biology/pages/1-2-the-process-of-science
- **Linear relationships and slope:** OpenStax *Introductory Statistics 2e*, Section 12.1 Linear Equations: https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/12-1-linear-equations

---

### Check answers

2. **It will decrease.** Fewer snakes means more frogs survive, and more frogs eat more insects.

## The Scientific Method and Experimental Design

**TEAS objective S.4.4** · Scientific Reasoning

**What the TEAS expects:** you can put the steps of the scientific method in order, identify the independent, dependent and controlled variables and the control group in an experiment, and spot flaws in an experimental design.

---

### 1. The steps

1. **Observe** and ask a **question**.
2. Do **background research**.
3. Form a **hypothesis**: a testable, falsifiable prediction (one that a result could prove wrong) (often "If…, then…").
4. Design and conduct an **experiment** to test it.
5. **Collect and analyze** the data.
6. Draw a **conclusion**: do the data support or refute the hypothesis?
7. **Communicate** the results so others can check and **replicate** them.

Real science loops: a refuted hypothesis leads to a new one. Data **support** or **refute** a hypothesis; they don't **prove** it.

---

### 2. Variables and groups

| Term | Meaning | In a fertilizer and plant-height experiment |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent variable** | The one factor the experimenter **changes on purpose** | Amount of fertilizer |
| **Dependent variable** | What is **measured**; it depends on the independent variable | Plant height |
| **Controlled variables (constants)** | Everything else, **kept the same** for all groups | Water, light, soil, pot size, plant type |
| **Control group** | Gets **no treatment** (or the standard one), for comparison | Plants with 0 g fertilizer |
| **Experimental group(s)** | Get the treatment | Plants with 5, 10 and 15 g |

**Memory hook:** the **I**ndependent variable is what **I** change; the **D**ependent variable is the **D**ata I collect.

In medical studies, the control group often gets a **placebo** (a fake treatment), so that expectations affect both groups equally.

> **Check 1:** A student tests whether music affects how fast mice run a maze. One group hears classical music, one hears rock, and one hears no music. Each mouse's time is recorded. Name the independent variable, the dependent variable and the control group.
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. What makes a good experiment

- **One** independent variable at a time, so you know what caused the change.
- A **control group** for comparison.
- **Controlled variables** kept constant.
- A **large enough sample** and **repeated trials**, which reduce the effect of chance.
- **Random assignment** to groups, so the groups start out alike.
- **Blinding** where possible, which keeps expectations from biasing results.

**Common design flaws:** changing two variables at once, no control group, a tiny sample, groups that differ before the experiment starts, or measuring in different ways for different groups.

> **Check 2:** A student wants to know whether a new fertilizer helps tomatoes grow. She gives the fertilizer to plants by a sunny window and no fertilizer to plants in a dark closet. What is wrong with this design?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Swapping independent and dependent variables.** Ask: which one did the experimenter choose?
- **Calling the control group a "constant."** The control group is a group; constants are conditions.
- **Placing the hypothesis after the experiment.** The hypothesis comes first; the experiment tests it.
- **Saying an experiment "proved" something.** It supports or refutes.

---

### Learn it properly

- **The scientific method:** OpenStax *Concepts of Biology*, Section 1.2 The Process of Science: https://openstax.org/books/concepts-biology/pages/1-2-the-process-of-science
- **More detail on hypotheses and experiments:** OpenStax *Biology 2e*, Section 1.1 The Science of Biology: https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/1-1-the-science-of-biology

---

### Check answers

1. **Independent variable:** the type of music. **Dependent variable:** the time to run the maze. **Control group:** the mice that hear no music.
2. **Two variables changed at once** (fertilizer and light), so any difference can't be blamed on the fertilizer. Both groups should get the same light.
