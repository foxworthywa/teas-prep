## Summarizing a Passage

**TEAS objective R.1.1** · Key Ideas and Details

**What the TEAS expects:** you can pick the statement that best sums up a passage of several paragraphs.

---

### 1. What a good summary does

A summary is a **short restatement of the main idea and the most important supporting points**, in your own words. A good summary:

- covers the **whole** passage, not just one paragraph;
- keeps the author's meaning (no new ideas, no opinions of your own);
- leaves out small details, examples, and numbers unless they are the point.

---

### 2. A fast method

1. Read the **first and last paragraphs** closely. Main ideas often appear there.
2. For each paragraph, think of a **three- to five-word label**. For an article about bees, the labels might be "how bees make honey," "bees pollinate crops," and "bee numbers falling."
3. Ask: **what do all the labels have in common?** That is the main idea.
4. Check each answer choice against the whole passage.

---

### 3. How wrong answers are built

| Wrong-answer type | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Too narrow** | A true detail from one paragraph |
| **Too broad** | A big claim the passage never makes |
| **Distorted** | Uses the passage's words but changes the meaning |
| **Contradicts** | States something the passage rejects |

> **Check 1:** A passage has three paragraphs: one on how bees make honey, one on how bees pollinate crops, and one on why bee populations are falling. Is "Bees pollinate many crops" a good summary? Why or why not?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 4. Summary vs. main idea vs. theme

- **Main idea:** the one point the passage makes (one sentence).
- **Summary:** the main idea plus the key supporting points (a few sentences, or one long one).
- **Theme:** a lesson about life that a story suggests ("telling the truth brings relief"). Themes are most common in stories; with nonfiction, compare main ideas and values instead (see R.3.2).

> **Check 2:** Which is the better summary of a story in which a girl loses a race, trains all winter, and wins in the spring? (a) "A girl runs in a race." (b) "After losing a race, a girl trains hard and wins the next one."
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Choosing a choice because it quotes the passage.** Exact words can still be distorted.
- **Choosing the most interesting detail.** A summary is about the whole.
- **Adding your own opinion.** "Bees are amazing" isn't in the passage.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Reading strategies, including summarizing:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 5.2 Effective Reading Strategies: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/5-2-effective-reading-strategies

---

### Check answers

1. **No.** (Any answer that says it covers only part of the passage is right.) It's too narrow: it covers only the second paragraph. A better summary mentions honey, pollination, and falling bee numbers.
2. **(b).** It covers the beginning, the key change, and the outcome. Choice (a) leaves out the training and the win.

## Making Inferences About a Text

**TEAS objective R.1.2** · Key Ideas and Details

**What the TEAS expects:** you can draw conclusions that a passage supports but doesn't state directly, including conclusions about its purpose and meaning.

---

### 1. What an inference is

Something **implied** is suggested but not said directly. An **inference** is a logical conclusion based on **evidence in the text plus reasoning**. It's not a wild guess, and it's not something stated word for word.

**Example:** *Maya checked the clock for the third time, tapped her foot, and sighed.*
The text never says "Maya is impatient," but the evidence supports that inference.

---

### 2. The evidence test

For each answer choice, ask: **"Which words in the passage prove this?"**

| Choice is… | Why |
|---|---|
| **Correct** | Clearly supported by specific details |
| **Too far** | Might be true, but goes beyond what the text supports |
| **Opposite** | Contradicted by a detail |
| **Unrelated** | About something the passage doesn't discuss |

The right inference is usually **modest**. Watch for extreme words such as *always, never, all, only,* or *proves*.

> **Check 1:** *The parking lot was empty, the lights were off, and a sign on the door read "Back Monday."* Which is the best inference: (a) the store went out of business, or (b) the store is closed for now?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Inferring a character's feelings or motives

Look at **actions, words, and reactions**. What a character does often says more than what the narrator states.

*"You noticed something the rest of us missed," the nurse said.* → The nurse values careful observation.

---

### 4. Inferring purpose and meaning

Ask why the author included a detail, a quotation, or an example. What would be lost if it were removed?

> **Check 2:** A health article about handwashing opens with a story about a child who caught the flu from a shared toy. What can you infer about why the author begins this way?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Using outside knowledge** instead of the passage.
- **Picking the most dramatic choice.** Strong inferences are usually cautious.
- **Confusing stated with implied.** If it's stated word for word, it's not an inference question's best answer, but it can still be the evidence.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Critical thinking and drawing conclusions:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 7.4 Critical Thinking: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/7-4-critical-thinking

---

### Check answers

1. **(b).** The sign says "Back Monday," so the store will reopen; nothing supports going out of business.
2. **To catch the reader's interest and show the topic matters in real life.** A specific story makes the risk feel personal.

## Following Written Directions

**TEAS objective R.1.3** · Key Ideas and Details

**What the TEAS expects:** you can read a set of directions, such as instructions, a label, or a procedure, and figure out exactly what a person should do in a given situation.

---

### 1. Read directions like a nurse reads an order

1. **Find the situation** in the question (who, how old, what happened, what time).
2. **Find the matching line** in the directions. Labels and instructions are often organized by age, dose, or step.
3. **Read the whole line, including exceptions.** Words such as *unless, except, only, before, after, do not,* and *ask a doctor* change everything.
4. **Pick the choice that matches exactly**, not the one that sounds most helpful.

---

### 2. Words that change the meaning

| Word or phrase | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **unless / except** | an exception to the rule |
| **before / after / within** | timing |
| **do not / never / stop** | a prohibition |
| **ask a doctor / call** | the person shouldn't decide alone |
| **every 24 hours** | how often (timing) |
| **not more than … in 24 hours** | a limit |

> **Check 1:** A label says: "Adults: 2 tablets every 6 hours. Do not take more than 8 tablets in 24 hours. Children under 12: ask a doctor." What should a 10-year-old's parent do?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Directions with steps

When directions are numbered, the order matters. If a question describes someone who did the steps in the wrong order or skipped one, find the **first** step that went wrong.

> **Check 2:** Directions: "1. Wash your hands. 2. Put on gloves. 3. Remove the old bandage. 4. Clean the wound." A student put on gloves, then washed her hands. What should she have done first?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 4. Directions that change something step by step

Some TEAS questions give a starting word or number and a list of changes. **Write the result after every step** on your scratch paper; don't try to hold it all in your head.

**Example:** Start with the word HEART.
1. Remove the last letter. → HEAR
2. Change the first letter to B. → BEAR
3. Add the letter D to the end. → BEARD

> **Check 3:** Start with the number 25. Double it. Subtract 10. Divide the result by 4. What number do you end with?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Answering from experience** instead of from the directions given.
- **Missing the age or weight group.** Check which line applies.
- **Ignoring "ask a doctor."** On the TEAS, if the label says to ask, the answer is to ask.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Active reading strategies:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 5.2 Effective Reading Strategies: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/5-2-effective-reading-strategies

---

### Check answers

1. **Ask a doctor.** The adult dose doesn't apply to children under 12.
2. **Washed her hands (step 1).** She put on gloves (step 2) first instead.
3. **10.** 25 × 2 = 50; 50 − 10 = 40; 40 ÷ 4 = 10.

## Locating Specific Information

**TEAS objective R.1.4** · Key Ideas and Details

**What the TEAS expects:** you can quickly find a specific fact, such as a date, a place, a number, or a name, in a passage, memo, or schedule.

---

### 1. Scan, don't reread

**Scanning** means moving your eyes quickly to find one piece of information without reading every word.

1. Decide **what kind of answer** you need: a date? a room number? a price?
2. Pick a **key word** from the question to look for.
3. Scan for the key word **or** for the shape of the answer (numbers, capital letters, dollar signs).
4. When you find it, **read the full sentence** around it to confirm.

---

### 2. Watch for near-misses

Passages often include **several similar facts**: two dates, three rooms, a deadline, and a start date. Wrong answers are usually real details from the passage that answer a **different** question.

**Example:** *Submit the form by March 1. Permits will be mailed starting March 5.*
"When is the form due?" → **March 1** (not March 5).

> **Check 1:** *The library opens at 8 a.m. on weekdays and 10 a.m. on Saturday. It closes at 9 p.m. Monday through Thursday and at 5 p.m. on Friday and Saturday.* When does the library close on Friday?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Use the text's structure

- **Headings and bold words** tell you where to look.
- **Lists and tables** line up facts; read across the correct row.
- **Memos** usually put who, what, and when in the first lines and exceptions near the end.

> **Check 2:** A memo says permits are picked up in Room 104 but forms are submitted online. A question asks where to pick up a permit. Which detail is the trap?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Grabbing the first number you see.**
- **Mixing up a deadline with a start date.**
- **Not confirming** the answer by reading the whole sentence.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Reading strategies, including skimming:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 5.2 Effective Reading Strategies: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/5-2-effective-reading-strategies

---

### Check answers

1. **5 p.m.** Friday is grouped with Saturday.
2. **"Online."** That's where forms go, not where permits are picked up.

## Reading Charts, Graphs, and Other Visuals

**TEAS objective R.1.5** · Key Ideas and Details

**What the TEAS expects:** you can read information from tables, graphs, schedules, labels, and diagrams, and use it to answer a question.

---

### 1. Read the frame first

Before you look at any numbers, read:

- the **title** (what the visual is about);
- the **labels** on rows, columns, or axes (what each number means);
- the **units** (percent? minutes? dollars?);
- any **key, legend, or notes** below the visual (such as "closed on weekends" or "fictional data").

---

### 2. Then find the exact cell

A **pie chart** shows parts of a whole: the slices add up to 100%, and a bigger slice means a bigger share.

For a table, put one finger on the **row** and one on the **column**; the answer is where they meet. For a bar or line graph, trace straight across to the axis.

> **Check 1:**
>
> | Day | Lab hours |
> |---|---|
> | Monday | 7–11 a.m. |
> | Tuesday | Closed |
> | Wednesday | 7–11 a.m. |
>
> Can a patient get a blood draw at 9 a.m. on Tuesday?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Questions that need two steps

Many visual questions ask you to **compare** or **combine**:

- **Difference:** subtract (41 minutes − 15 minutes = 26 minutes more).
- **Meets a goal?** Compare each value to the goal, and watch for "at least" (≥) versus "more than" (>).
- **Pattern:** look at the direction of change (rising, falling, no clear pattern).
- **Two services on one day:** find the days that are open for **both**.

> **Check 2:** A goal is "at least 70%." The groups scored 28%, 52%, and 70%. How many groups met the goal?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Reading the wrong row or column.** Slow down and trace.
- **Ignoring the notes** under a table.
- **Reporting a total when the question asks for a difference** (or the reverse).

---

### Learn it properly

- **Bar graphs and line graphs:** OpenStax *Introductory Statistics 2e*, Section 2.1 Stem-and-Leaf Graphs (Stemplots), Line Graphs, and Bar Graphs: https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/2-1-stem-and-leaf-graphs-stemplots-line-graphs-and-bar-graphs
- **More practice with tables and graphs:** see the M.2.1 study page.

---

### Check answers

1. **No.** The lab is closed on Tuesday.
2. **One.** Only 70% is "at least 70%."

## Interpreting Events in Sequence

**TEAS objective R.1.6** · Key Ideas and Details

**What the TEAS expects:** you can put events or steps in order and tell what happened before, after, or immediately after something else.

---

### 1. Signal words

| Signal | Examples |
|---|---|
| Order | first, second, next, then, finally, last |
| Time | before, after, while, during, later, meanwhile, by the time |
| Dates and clock times | In 1854…, At eleven o'clock… |

---

### 2. Text order isn't always time order

Writers sometimes **flash back** or mention a later event early. Use dates and signal words, not just the order of the sentences.

*In 2024 Rosa graduated from nursing school. Four years earlier, she had started college.* → Starting college (2020) came **first**, even though the sentence about it comes second.

> **Check 1:** *Before the meeting, Sam printed the agenda. After the meeting ended, he emailed the notes. During the meeting, he took attendance.* What did Sam do second?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. "Immediately after" questions

"Immediately after" means the **very next** event, not just something later. Make a quick list:

1. Resident explains the problem
2. Aide reports it to the nurse
3. Nurse calls the kitchen
4. Food arrives

What happened immediately after step 1? **Step 2**, not step 3 or 4.

---

### 4. Ordering steps in directions

For process questions, ask what **must** happen first for the next step to work (you can't rinse off soap you haven't applied).

> **Check 2:** Put these handwashing steps in order: (A) Rinse your hands. (B) Wet your hands. (C) Dry your hands. (D) Lather with soap and scrub.
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Assuming the first sentence describes the first event.**
- **Treating "after" as "immediately after."**
- **Skipping a step** when building a sequence.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Reading strategies:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 5.2 Effective Reading Strategies: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/5-2-effective-reading-strategies

---

### Check answers

1. **Took attendance.** Before the meeting (printed), during (attendance), after (emailed).
2. **B, D, A, C.** Wet, lather and scrub, rinse, dry.

## Fact, Opinion, Bias, and Misconceptions

**TEAS objective R.2.1** · Craft and Structure

**What the TEAS expects:** you can tell facts from opinions and spot bias, stereotypes, and misconceptions in a text.

A **fact** can be checked. An **opinion** is a belief or judgment. **Bias** is a one-sided slant.

---

### 1. Fact or opinion?

| Fact | Opinion |
|---|---|
| Can be **checked or measured** | Expresses a **judgment, belief, or feeling** |
| *A 20-ounce soda has about 16 teaspoons of sugar.* | *Selling soda in a hospital is ridiculous.* |
| *The clinic opens at 8 a.m.* | *The clinic should open earlier.* |

**Words that often signal opinion:** *should, best, worst, amazing, terrible, ridiculous, I believe.* They are clues, not proof; read the whole statement. ("Patients must fast for 12 hours" is a rule that can be checked.)

A statement of fact can be checked, and checking might show it is false. "The moon is 50 miles away" is a factual claim; checking shows it is wrong.

> **Check 1:** Fact or opinion? (a) "The flu shot is offered every fall." (b) "Everyone who skips a flu shot is careless."
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Spotting bias

**Bias** is a one-sided slant that ignores or misrepresents other views. Watch for:

- **Overgeneralizations:** *every, all, always, never* applied to people ("Every administrator only cares about money").
- **Loaded words** that carry strong feelings (*greedy, heroic, disaster*).
- **Missing evidence:** big claims with no support.
- **Who benefits:** a company praising its own product.

---

### 3. Stereotypes

A **stereotype** is a fixed, oversimplified belief about everyone in a group: "Older patients can't learn new technology," or "Teenagers are lazy." Stereotypes ignore differences between individuals, so they are a form of bias. On the TEAS, a choice that judges a whole group by one trait is usually the biased one.

> **Check 2:** Which statement is a stereotype? (a) "Some of our patients prefer printed instructions." (b) "Older people never understand online forms."
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 4. Misconceptions

A **misconception** is a common but mistaken belief. Texts, especially ads, sometimes rely on one:

- "Natural means safe." (Many natural substances are harmful.)
- "If it worked for one person, it will work for me." (One story isn't evidence.)
- "More is better." (Doses have limits.)

> **Check 3:** An ad says, "Our tea is all natural, so there are no side effects." What misconception does it rely on?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Calling a statement an opinion because you disagree with it.**
- **Calling a statement a fact just because it includes a number.** Ask whether it can actually be checked and where the number came from.
- **Missing quiet bias** in word choice.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Evaluating information and recognizing bias:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 7.4 Critical Thinking: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/7-4-critical-thinking

---

### Check answers

1. **(a) fact; (b) opinion.** (b) is a judgment and an overgeneralization.
2. **(b).** It judges all older people by one trait; (a) says only "some."
3. **That "natural" means "safe."** Natural products can still cause side effects.

## Word Meaning from Context

**TEAS objective R.2.2** · Craft and Structure

**What the TEAS expects:** you can figure out what a word or phrase means from the sentences around it.

---

### 1. Kinds of context clues

| Clue | Example | Meaning of the bold word |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | ***Fasting***, or going without food, is required before the test. | going without food |
| **Example** | ***Citrus*** fruits, such as oranges, lemons, and limes, are high in vitamin C. | a group of sour, juicy fruits |
| **Contrast** | Online tutoring is not a ***luxury***; it is a necessity. | something nice but not necessary |
| **Restatement or explanation** | The wound was ***superficial***; it barely broke the skin. | on the surface, not deep |
| **Cause and effect** | Because the medicine works only ***temporarily***, the dose is repeated daily. | for a short time |

---

### 2. The substitution test

1. Cover the word.
2. Think of your own word that fits the sentence.
3. Plug each answer choice into the sentence. Keep the one that **fits the meaning**, not just the grammar.

> **Check 1:** *The new aide was meticulous; she checked every chart twice and never missed a detail.* What does **meticulous** most nearly mean?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Words with more than one meaning

Many test words are common words used in a **less common sense**. "Humble" can describe a modest person, but in *the humble potato feeds much of the world* it means **ordinary**. Always choose the meaning that fits **this** sentence.

> **Check 2:** *The report was dry, full of tables and no stories.* Does **dry** mean "not wet" here?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 4. Word parts help too

Prefixes, roots, and suffixes (see the E.3.2 study page) can confirm a guess: *temporarily* → *tempor* (time) + *-ary* (relating to) + *-ly* (in a way) → for a time.

---

### 5. Connotation

Words with similar meanings can carry different feelings. *Thrifty* and *cheap* both mean spending little, but *thrifty* sounds positive and *cheap* sounds negative. When a question asks what a word suggests, think about the feeling as well as the meaning.

---

### Common traps

- **Choosing the most common meaning** instead of the meaning in context.
- **Choosing a choice related to the topic** but not to the word.
- **Not rereading the sentence** with your answer in place.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Reading strategies, including vocabulary:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 5.2 Effective Reading Strategies: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/5-2-effective-reading-strategies

---

### Check answers

1. **Very careful and precise.** The clue is checking every chart twice.
2. **No.** Here it means dull or uninteresting.

## The Author's Purpose

**TEAS objective R.2.3** · Craft and Structure

**What the TEAS expects:** you can tell why an author wrote a text, or why the author included a particular detail, example, or sentence.

---

### 1. The main purposes

| Purpose | Clues | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Inform** (explain or teach) | facts, definitions, neutral tone | textbook section, news report |
| **Persuade** (convince) | opinions, *should*, reasons, a **call to action** (telling the reader to do something) | editorial, ad, letter to the editor |
| **Entertain** (tell a story or amuse) | characters, plot, humor, description | short story, personal narrative |
| **Instruct** (tell how to do something) | numbered steps, commands | directions, recipe, medication label |

Some texts mix purposes; pick the **main** one.

> **Check 1:** A pamphlet lists "five reasons you should quit smoking today" and ends with a phone number for a support line. What is its main purpose?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Why is this detail here?

Questions often ask why the author included **one** part. Common answers:

- to give **evidence** for a claim (a statistic, a study);
- to give an **example** that makes an idea concrete;
- to **grab attention** (an opening story, a surprising fact, a question to the reader);
- to answer a **counterargument**;
- to show a **cause and effect** chain.

**Test:** imagine the detail removed. What would the reader lose?

> **Check 2:** An article says oysters filter the water, and the cleaner water lets sunlight reach underwater grasses that shelter young crabs. Why does the author mention the grasses?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Choosing "inform" for every text with facts.** Ads and editorials use facts to persuade.
- **Confusing the topic with the purpose.** "Oysters" is the topic; "to explain why oysters matter" is the purpose.
- **Overreading** a light or funny detail as the main purpose.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Analytical thinking:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 7.3 Analytical Thinking: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/7-3-analytical-thinking

---

### Check answers

1. **To persuade.** "Should," a list of reasons, and a call to action.
2. **To show how cleaner water from oysters benefits other living things.**

## Point of View and Perspective

**TEAS objective R.2.4** · Craft and Structure

**What the TEAS expects:** you can identify who is telling a text (the **narrator**), how the author feels about the subject, and how the author's background shapes the view.

---

### 1. Narrative point of view

| Point of view | Pronouns | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **First person** | I, me, we | *I clocked in twenty minutes early.* |
| **Second person** | you | *You clock in and grab a clipboard.* |
| **Third person** | he, she, they, names | *Dana clocked in twenty minutes early.* |

Count the pronouns **outside of quotation marks**: dialogue can use "I" even in a third-person story.

---

### 2. The author's attitude (tone)

**Tone** is the attitude an author shows toward the subject.

Word choice reveals how the author feels:

| Attitude | Clue words |
|---|---|
| Approving, enthusiastic | *excellent, promising, vital* |
| Disapproving, critical | *ridiculous, careless, failure* |
| Neutral, objective | facts and plain description, few feeling words |
| Cautious, concerned | *the details matter, should study first, may, might* |

> **Check 1:** *"Later start times sound good, but the details matter."* Is the author enthusiastic, opposed, or cautious?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. Background shapes perspective

An author's job, experience, or interests affect **what they notice and care about**. A pediatrician writes about health; a bus company owner writes about buses and costs. Neither is "wrong"; each has a perspective.

Questions may ask:
- Which statement would the author **most likely agree** with?
- How does the author's background **shape** the argument?

To answer, list the author's main concerns, then match.

> **Check 2:** A dentist writes an article about sports drinks. Which concern is she most likely to emphasize: (a) tooth decay from sugar, or (b) the price of the drinks?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Confusing the author with a character** in a story.
- **Deciding the author's view from one sentence.** Look at the whole text.
- **Projecting your own opinion** onto the author.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Thinking critically about perspectives:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 7.4 Critical Thinking: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/7-4-critical-thinking

---

### Check answers

2. **(a).** A dentist's background makes tooth health the likely focus.

## Using Evidence to Predict and Conclude

**TEAS objective R.3.1** · Integration of Knowledge and Ideas

**What the TEAS expects:** you can use details from a text to predict what will likely happen, draw conclusions, and pick the evidence that best supports a conclusion.

---

### 1. Predictions follow the text's logic

A good prediction **continues a pattern or cause-and-effect chain** the text already describes.

*Oysters filter water → clearer water → more sunlight → more underwater grass.*
If more oysters are added, predict **clearer water and more grass**.

For characters, predict from **past behavior**: someone who fixed a failure once by studying it will likely do so again.

> **Check 1:** A passage explains that a town's new bike lanes led to fewer car crashes in the first year. If the town adds bike lanes to more streets, what is the most reasonable prediction?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Choosing the best evidence

When a question asks which sentence **best supports** a conclusion:

1. Restate the conclusion in your own words.
2. Find the choice that **directly** proves it, not one that is just on the same topic.
3. Prefer specific evidence (data, a direct statement) over general background.

> **Check 2:** Conclusion: "Exercise improves mood." Which is better evidence: (a) "Many people exercise in the morning," or (b) "In a study, people who walked daily reported less anxiety"?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. How far is too far?

| Choice | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Follows from the text | ✓ |
| Possible, but the text gives no support | ✗ |
| Uses outside knowledge | ✗ |
| Says *always, proves, never* when the text is cautious | ✗ |

---

### Common traps

- **Predicting what you would like to happen** instead of what the text supports.
- **Choosing evidence that repeats the topic** without supporting the claim.
- **Ignoring time limits** in the text (for example, "takes two to three years").

---

### Learn it properly

- **Drawing conclusions from evidence:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 7.4 Critical Thinking: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/7-4-critical-thinking

---

### Check answers

1. **Crashes will likely drop on those streets too.** The prediction continues the cause and effect the passage describes.
2. **(b).** It links exercise to mood with data; (a) is only about timing.

## Comparing Themes Across Texts

**TEAS objective R.3.2** · Integration of Knowledge and Ideas

**What the TEAS expects:** you can identify the theme or central message of one or more texts and compare how texts treat similar ideas.

---

### 1. What a theme is

A **theme** is the message or lesson about life that a story suggests. It's a **complete thought**, not a single word. With nonfiction, such as opinion pieces, compare **main ideas** and **values** (beliefs about what is important) instead.

- Topic: *failure* (one word)
- Theme: *Failure can teach you how to succeed.* (a lesson)

Find the theme by asking: **What does the main character learn or change? What happens because of it?**

> **Check 1:** In a story, a boy hides a broken window from his parents, feels worse every day, and finally tells the truth and feels relieved. What is the theme?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Comparing two texts

| Step | Ask |
|---|---|
| 1 | What is each text's main idea or theme? |
| 2 | Where do they **agree**? (shared theme, shared value) |
| 3 | Where do they **differ**? (focus, attitude, how the character reaches the lesson) |

Two stories can share a theme but reach it differently: one character figures things out alone; another resists advice first.

Two **opinion pieces** can disagree on a solution yet share a value (both care about students), or focus on different concerns (health vs. cost).

> **Check 2:** Passage A argues for later school start times to protect teens' health. Passage B worries that paying for extra buses would take money away from teachers and student programs. What value do both authors share?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Naming a topic instead of a theme.**
- **Choosing a theme that fits only one of the texts.**
- **Mixing up which passage said what** in comparison questions. Label each passage's main point before reading the choices.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Analytical thinking:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 7.3 Analytical Thinking: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/7-3-analytical-thinking

---

### Check answers

1. **Telling the truth, even when it's hard, brings relief** (or: honesty is better than hiding mistakes).
2. **Concern for students:** A cares about their health; B cares about their teachers and programs.

## Evaluating an Argument

**TEAS objective R.3.3** · Integration of Knowledge and Ideas

**What the TEAS expects:** you can find an author's claim and evidence, judge how strong the evidence is, and spot weak reasoning.

---

### 1. Parts of an argument

- **Claim:** the main point the author wants you to accept.
- **Reasons and evidence:** facts, data, studies, examples, and expert statements that support the claim.
- **Counterargument and rebuttal:** an opposing view and the author's answer to it.

> **Check 1:** *"Colleges should offer online tutoring all night. At one college, students who used it at least three times were more likely to pass math."* What is the claim, and what is the evidence?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 2. Strong vs. weak support

| Stronger | Weaker |
|---|---|
| Data from a study or survey | One person's story (a testimonial) |
| Expert or official sources | Unnamed "experts" or "everyone knows" |
| Evidence that directly fits the claim | Evidence about a related but different topic |
| Balanced, fair language | Insults, exaggeration, overgeneralization |

---

### 3. Common reasoning problems

- **Overgeneralization:** "Every administrator only cares about profit."
- **Personal attack:** attacking the person instead of the idea.
- **Testimonial only:** "Jamie from Ohio loves it!"
- **Correlation treated as cause:** two things happen together, so one must cause the other.
- **Either/or:** pretending there are only two choices.

> **Check 2:** "Students who eat breakfast get better grades, so breakfast makes students smarter." What is the reasoning problem?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 4. Strengthen or weaken?

To **strengthen** an argument, add evidence that supports the author's **claim** or main reason. To **weaken** it, add evidence that contradicts the claim or shows another cause.

*Example:* The claim is "Later start times reduce teen car crashes." A study showing fewer crashes after start times changed would **strengthen** it; a study showing crashes fell everywhere, even where start times stayed the same, would **weaken** it.

---

### Common traps

- **Agreeing with the claim** and then calling the evidence strong.
- **Treating every number as strong evidence.** Ask where it came from and whether it fits the claim.
- **Missing the rebuttal.** Authors often answer critics with "However…"

---

### Learn it properly

- **Evaluating claims and evidence:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 7.4 Critical Thinking: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/7-4-critical-thinking

---

### Check answers

1. **Claim:** colleges should offer all-night online tutoring. **Evidence:** the finding that, at one college, students who used it were more likely to pass math.
2. **Correlation treated as cause.** Other factors (sleep, family support) could explain both.

## Integrating Multiple Sources

**TEAS objective R.3.4** · Integration of Knowledge and Ideas

**What the TEAS expects:** you can combine information from two or more sources, such as an article and a table, directions and a schedule, or two viewpoints, and judge how they fit together.

---

### 1. Read each source for its job

| Source type | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| Article or essay | claims, explanations, advice |
| Table, chart, or graph | numbers and patterns |
| Directions or label | rules and limits |
| Schedule or calendar | when and where |
| Ad or opinion piece | a viewpoint, possibly biased |

Before answering, write a few words for each source: *Source 1: no screens before bed. Source 2: more screen time = less sleep.*

---

### 2. How sources relate

- **Support:** the data back up the article's advice.
- **Contradict:** one source says the opposite.
- **Add:** one source gives a detail the other lacks.
- **Combine:** you need both to answer (for example, a rule from the directions plus an open day from the schedule).

> **Check 1:** Directions say to fast (not eat) for 12 hours before a blood test. The lab opens at 7 a.m. and is open only Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. It is now Tuesday at noon, and a patient wants the earliest possible test. When should she stop eating?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### 3. What data can and can't show

A survey or table shows **what happened together** (a correlation). By itself, it can't prove that one thing **caused** another. If students who used screens longer slept less, screens *might* be the cause, but stress or late-night jobs could explain both.

> **Check 2:** A table shows that towns with more ice cream sales have more sunburns. Does ice cream cause sunburn?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

### Common traps

- **Answering from only one source** when the question needs both.
- **Treating a correlation as proof of cause.**
- **Trusting a biased source** (an ad) over a neutral one without checking.

---

### Learn it properly

- **Evaluating information from different sources:** OpenStax *College Success*, Section 7.4 Critical Thinking: https://openstax.org/books/college-success/pages/7-4-critical-thinking
- **Reading graphs:** see the R.1.5 and M.2.1 study pages.

---

### Check answers

1. **By 7 p.m. Tuesday.** The next open day is Wednesday at 7 a.m., and 12 hours before that is 7 p.m. Tuesday.
2. **No.** Both rise in hot, sunny summer weather, when people buy more ice cream and spend more time in the sun. Sunburn is caused by the sun's UV rays, not by ice cream.
