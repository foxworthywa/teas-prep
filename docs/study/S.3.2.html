What the TEAS expects: you can describe solids, liquids and gases at the particle level, name each change of state, and tell physical properties and changes from chemical ones.

1. The three common states

Solid Liquid Gas
Shape Fixed Takes the shape of its container Fills its container
Volume Fixed Fixed Expands to fill the container
Particles Tightly packed; vibrate in place Close together; slide past each other Far apart; move fast and freely
Energy of particles Lowest Middle Highest
Compressible? No Barely Yes

A fourth state, plasma, is an extremely hot, ionized gas (stars, lightning).

2. Changes of state

Swipe the table sideways to see every column. →

Change From → To Energy
Melting Solid → liquid Absorbed
Freezing Liquid → solid Released
Vaporization (evaporation or boiling) Liquid → gas Absorbed
Condensation Gas → liquid Released
Sublimation Solid → gas directly Absorbed
Deposition Gas → solid directly Released
  • Examples: dry ice sublimes; frost forming on a window is deposition; water droplets on a cold glass are condensation.
  • Evaporation happens at the surface at any temperature; boiling happens throughout the liquid at the boiling point.
  • During a change of state, the temperature stays constant until the change is complete: the energy goes into breaking or forming attractions between particles, not into raising temperature. Ice water stays at 0 °C until all the ice melts.
  • Water's normal freezing/melting point is 0 °C (32 °F); its normal boiling point is 100 °C (212 °F) at sea level.

Memory hook: going toward gas absorbs energy (you have to add heat); going toward solid releases it.

3. Physical vs chemical

Physical properties can be observed without changing what the substance is: color, density, melting point, boiling point, hardness, solubility, conductivity.

Chemical properties describe how a substance reacts to form new substances: flammability, reactivity with acid or oxygen, tendency to rust.

Physical change (same substance) Chemical change (new substance)
Melting, freezing, boiling Burning
Cutting, crushing, bending Rusting
Dissolving sugar in water Cooking or baking
Mixing sand and iron filings Digesting food
Breaking glass Milk souring, fruit rotting

Signs of a chemical change: a gas forms (bubbles), a color change, a precipitate (solid) forms from two liquids, light or a temperature change is produced, or an odor appears. These signs suggest, but don't prove, a chemical change: boiling also makes bubbles.

4. Special properties of water

  • Cohesion: water molecules stick to each other (hydrogen bonds), which causes surface tension.
  • Adhesion: water sticks to other surfaces, which helps it climb narrow tubes (capillary action) and forms the meniscus.
  • High specific heat: water absorbs a lot of heat with little temperature change, which keeps body temperature and climates stable.
  • Good solvent: water is polar, so it dissolves many substances.

5. Density

Density = mass ÷ volume (usually g/mL or g/cm³).

  • An object less dense than a liquid floats in it; one more dense sinks.
  • Water's density is 1 g/mL. Ice is less dense than liquid water (about 0.92 g/mL), which is why ice floats. That is unusual: most substances are denser as solids.

Check 2: A 40-gram rock has a volume of 10 mL. What is its density, and will it float in water? Answer at the bottom of the page.

Common traps

  • Calling a change of state chemical. Melting, freezing and boiling are physical.
  • Thinking temperature rises during melting. It stays constant until melting is complete.
  • Mixing up sublimation and deposition. Sublimation goes from a solid; deposition goes to a solid.
  • Assuming bubbles always mean a chemical change. Boiling water bubbles too.
