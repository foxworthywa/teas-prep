TEAS objective S.4.1 · Scientific Reasoning

What the TEAS expects: you can use metric units and prefixes, convert between them, pick the right tool for a measurement, read instruments correctly, and tell accuracy from precision.

1. Base units (metric / SI)

Swipe the table sideways to see every column. →

Quantity Base unit Tool
Length meter (m) Ruler, meter stick
Mass gram (g) (the SI base unit is the kilogram) Balance (triple-beam or electronic)
Volume of a liquid liter (L) Graduated cylinder, pipette, volumetric flask (beakers and Erlenmeyer flasks have only rough markings; don't use them to measure)
Temperature degrees Celsius (°C); kelvin (K) in science Thermometer
Time second (s) Stopwatch

Mass vs weight: mass is the amount of matter (measured with a balance; the same on the Moon). Weight is the pull of gravity on that mass (measured with a scale; less on the Moon).

Volume of a solid: 1 mL = 1 cm³ (cubic centimeter, also written cc). For an irregular object, use water displacement: the rise in water level in a graduated cylinder equals the object's volume.

2. Metric prefixes

Swipe the table sideways to see every column. →

Prefix Symbol Meaning
kilo- k 1,000 × base
hecto- h 100 × base
deka- da 10 × base
(base unit) 1
deci- d 1/10 of base
centi- c 1/100 of base
milli- m 1/1,000 of base
micro- μ (or mc in medicine) 1/1,000,000 of base: 3 steps below milli, so 1 mg = 1,000 mcg

Memory hook (largest to smallest): King Henry Died By Drinking Chocolate Milk = kilo, hecto, deka, Base, deci, centi, milli. Each step from kilo down to milli is a factor of 10. Micro is a jump of 1,000 below milli, not 10.

Converting: moving to a smaller unit gives a bigger number (multiply); moving to a larger unit gives a smaller number (divide). Count the steps and move the decimal point that many places.

  • 2.5 kg → g: 3 steps smaller → 2,500 g
  • 450 mg → g: 3 steps larger → 0.45 g
  • 35 mm → cm: 1 step larger → 3.5 cm
  • 0.5 mg → mcg: micro is 1,000 times smaller → 500 mcg

3. Temperature

  • Water freezes at 0 °C (32 °F) and boils at 100 °C (212 °F). Normal body temperature is about 37 °C (98.6 °F).
  • °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32 and °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9
  • Worked example: a fever of 104 °F → (104 − 32) × 5/9 = 72 × 5/9 = 40 °C.
  • K = °C + 273 (0 K is absolute zero; there are no negative kelvin temperatures).

4. Reading instruments

  • Read a graduated cylinder at the bottom of the meniscus (the curved water surface), with your eye level with it. Looking from above or below gives a wrong reading (parallax error).
  • Read one digit beyond the smallest marking by estimating between lines. If a cylinder is marked every 1 mL and the meniscus is halfway between 36 and 37, record 36.5 mL.
  • Zero (tare) the balance with the empty container on it, so the reading shows only the sample. (If you don't tare, subtract the container's mass instead; never do both.)

5. Accuracy vs precision

  • Accuracy: how close a measurement is to the true value.
  • Precision: how close repeated measurements are to each other.

Think of a dartboard: darts tightly grouped but far from the bullseye are precise but not accurate. Darts scattered evenly around the bullseye are accurate on average but not precise. A miscalibrated scale that always reads 2 kg high gives precise but inaccurate results.

Check 2: A student weighs the same 10.0 g sample three times and gets 12.1 g, 12.0 g and 12.1 g. Are the results accurate, precise, both, or neither? Answer at the bottom of the page.

Common traps

  • Moving the decimal the wrong way. Smaller unit → bigger number.
  • Confusing mass and weight. A balance measures mass.
  • Reading the top of the meniscus. Read the bottom, at eye level.
  • Treating precise as accurate. Consistent results can all be wrong.
