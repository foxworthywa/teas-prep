TEAS objective S.4.4 · Scientific Reasoning

What the TEAS expects: you can put the steps of the scientific method in order, identify the independent, dependent and controlled variables and the control group in an experiment, and spot flaws in an experimental design.

1. The steps

  1. Observe and ask a question.
  2. Do background research.
  3. Form a hypothesis: a testable, falsifiable prediction (one that a result could prove wrong) (often "If…, then…").
  4. Design and conduct an experiment to test it.
  5. Collect and analyze the data.
  6. Draw a conclusion: do the data support or refute the hypothesis?
  7. Communicate the results so others can check and replicate them.

Real science loops: a refuted hypothesis leads to a new one. Data support or refute a hypothesis; they don't prove it.

2. Variables and groups

Swipe the table sideways to see every column. →

Term Meaning In a fertilizer and plant-height experiment
Independent variable The one factor the experimenter changes on purpose Amount of fertilizer
Dependent variable What is measured; it depends on the independent variable Plant height
Controlled variables (constants) Everything else, kept the same for all groups Water, light, soil, pot size, plant type
Control group Gets no treatment (or the standard one), for comparison Plants with 0 g fertilizer
Experimental group(s) Get the treatment Plants with 5, 10 and 15 g

Memory hook: the Independent variable is what I change; the Dependent variable is the Data I collect.

In medical studies, the control group often gets a placebo (a fake treatment), so that expectations affect both groups equally.

Check 1: A student tests whether music affects how fast mice run a maze. One group hears classical music, one hears rock, and one hears no music. Each mouse's time is recorded. Name the independent variable, the dependent variable and the control group. Answer at the bottom of the page.

3. What makes a good experiment

  • One independent variable at a time, so you know what caused the change.
  • A control group for comparison.
  • Controlled variables kept constant.
  • A large enough sample and repeated trials, which reduce the effect of chance.
  • Random assignment to groups, so the groups start out alike.
  • Blinding where possible, which keeps expectations from biasing results.

Common design flaws: changing two variables at once, no control group, a tiny sample, groups that differ before the experiment starts, or measuring in different ways for different groups.

Check 2: A student wants to know whether a new fertilizer helps tomatoes grow. She gives the fertilizer to plants by a sunny window and no fertilizer to plants in a dark closet. What is wrong with this design? Answer at the bottom of the page.

Common traps

  • Swapping independent and dependent variables. Ask: which one did the experimenter choose?
  • Calling the control group a "constant." The control group is a group; constants are conditions.
  • Placing the hypothesis after the experiment. The hypothesis comes first; the experiment tests it.
  • Saying an experiment "proved" something. It supports or refutes.
