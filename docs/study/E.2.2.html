TEAS objective E.2.2 · Knowledge of Language

What the TEAS expects: you can judge whether wording fits its purpose and audience, choosing formal or informal language and plain or technical terms as the situation requires.

1. Formal vs informal

Formal (essays, reports, emails to instructors or employers) Informal (texts and notes to friends)
Complete sentences, standard spelling Fragments, abbreviations (u, pls, ASAP)
Precise words: support, demonstrate, significant Slang: a big deal, cool with, stuff
Polite requests: Could you please… (direct instructions are fine in clinical orders and procedures) Blunt demands: Send me…
Usually no contractions in very formal writing Contractions are fine
First person (I) is fine in letters and emails; reports usually use third person. No emojis Any person, emojis

Ask: Who will read this? Why am I writing? What does the reader already know?

Purpose: most writing aims to inform (explain facts), persuade (convince the reader), or entertain. A lab report informs; a scholarship essay persuades.

Connotation: words with the same dictionary meaning (denotation) can carry different feelings. Thrifty sounds positive; cheap sounds negative. In professional writing, usually choose words with a neutral connotation.

Check 1: Rewrite for an email to an instructor: "Hey, gonna miss class, can u send the slides?" Answer at the bottom of the page.

2. Plain language vs technical terms

  • With patients and the public: use plain words and explain terms. "We'll put a small tube in a vein in your arm to give you fluids."
  • With other health professionals: precise technical terms save time and avoid confusion. "Start a peripheral IV and infuse normal saline at 100 mL/hr."
  • The right choice depends on the reader, not on which sounds smarter.

3. Tone and bias

  • Keep tone respectful and objective in professional writing: avoid sarcasm, exaggeration, and emotional wording.
  • Use person-first or preferred language: a patient with diabetes, not a diabetic in most clinical writing.
  • Avoid unnecessary references to gender, age, or other traits.

Check 2: Which is better in a lab report: "The results were super weird" or "The results were unexpected"? Answer at the bottom of the page.

Common traps

  • Assuming formal means complicated. Formal writing is clear and precise, not wordy.
  • Using jargon with a general audience.
  • Slang hiding in essays: kind of, a lot of stuff, gonna, totally.
