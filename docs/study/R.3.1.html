TEAS objective R.3.1 · Integration of Knowledge and Ideas

What the TEAS expects: you can use details from a text to predict what will likely happen, draw conclusions, and pick the evidence that best supports a conclusion.

1. Predictions follow the text's logic

A good prediction continues a pattern or cause-and-effect chain the text already describes.

Oysters filter water → clearer water → more sunlight → more underwater grass. If more oysters are added, predict clearer water and more grass.

For characters, predict from past behavior: someone who fixed a failure once by studying it will likely do so again.

Check 1: A passage explains that a town's new bike lanes led to fewer car crashes in the first year. If the town adds bike lanes to more streets, what is the most reasonable prediction? Answer at the bottom of the page.

2. Choosing the best evidence

When a question asks which sentence best supports a conclusion:

  1. Restate the conclusion in your own words.
  2. Find the choice that directly proves it, not one that is just on the same topic.
  3. Prefer specific evidence (data, a direct statement) over general background.

Check 2: Conclusion: "Exercise improves mood." Which is better evidence: (a) "Many people exercise in the morning," or (b) "In a study, people who walked daily reported less anxiety"? Answer at the bottom of the page.

3. How far is too far?

Choice Verdict
Follows from the text ✓
Possible, but the text gives no support ✗
Uses outside knowledge ✗
Says always, proves, never when the text is cautious ✗

Common traps

  • Predicting what you would like to happen instead of what the text supports.
  • Choosing evidence that repeats the topic without supporting the claim.
  • Ignoring time limits in the text (for example, "takes two to three years").
