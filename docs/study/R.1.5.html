TEAS objective R.1.5 · Key Ideas and Details

What the TEAS expects: you can read information from tables, graphs, schedules, labels, and diagrams, and use it to answer a question.

1. Read the frame first

Before you look at any numbers, read:

  • the title (what the visual is about);
  • the labels on rows, columns, or axes (what each number means);
  • the units (percent? minutes? dollars?);
  • any key, legend, or notes below the visual (such as "closed on weekends" or "fictional data").

2. Then find the exact cell

A pie chart shows parts of a whole: the slices add up to 100%, and a bigger slice means a bigger share.

For a table, put one finger on the row and one on the column; the answer is where they meet. For a bar or line graph, trace straight across to the axis.

Check 1:

Day Lab hours
Monday 7–11 a.m.
Tuesday Closed
Wednesday 7–11 a.m.

Can a patient get a blood draw at 9 a.m. on Tuesday? Answer at the bottom of the page.

3. Questions that need two steps

Many visual questions ask you to compare or combine:

  • Difference: subtract (41 minutes − 15 minutes = 26 minutes more).
  • Meets a goal? Compare each value to the goal, and watch for "at least" (≥) versus "more than" (>).
  • Pattern: look at the direction of change (rising, falling, no clear pattern).
  • Two services on one day: find the days that are open for both.

Check 2: A goal is "at least 70%." The groups scored 28%, 52%, and 70%. How many groups met the goal? Answer at the bottom of the page.

Common traps

  • Reading the wrong row or column. Slow down and trace.
  • Ignoring the notes under a table.
  • Reporting a total when the question asks for a difference (or the reverse).
