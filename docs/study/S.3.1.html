Atoms and the Periodic Table

TEAS objective S.3.1 · Chemistry

What the TEAS expects: you can describe the parts of an atom, use atomic number and mass number, explain isotopes and ions, and use the periodic table's layout to predict how elements behave and bond.

1. The parts of an atom

Particle Charge Where Mass
Proton +1 Nucleus About 1 atomic mass unit (amu)
Neutron 0 Nucleus About 1 amu
Electron −1 Outside the nucleus, in energy levels (shells) Almost none (about 1/1,800 amu)
  • Atomic number = number of protons. It defines the element: every atom with 6 protons is carbon.
  • Mass number = protons + neutrons. Electrons are too light to count.
  • In a neutral atom, electrons = protons.

Worked example: chlorine-35 has atomic number 17. Protons = 17, electrons (if neutral) = 17, neutrons = 35 − 17 = 18.

Check 1: An atom has atomic number 8 and mass number 17. How many neutrons does it have? Answer at the bottom of the page.

2. Isotopes and ions

  • Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons, so different mass numbers. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have 6 protons; carbon-14 has 8 neutrons instead of 6.
  • Ions are atoms that have gained or lost electrons, so they carry a charge.
    • Lose electrons → positive ion (cation), e.g. Na⁺.
    • Gain electrons → negative ion (anion), e.g. Cl⁻.
  • The atomic mass shown on the periodic table (e.g. 35.45 for chlorine) is a weighted average of the element's isotopes, which is why it is not a whole number.

Memory hook: cations are "pawsitive"; anions are negative.

3. Reading the periodic table

  • Elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number.
  • Groups (vertical columns) contain elements with the same number of valence electrons (for groups 1–2, the group number; for groups 13–18, subtract 10: oxygen in group 16 has 6) (outer-shell electrons), so they react similarly.
  • Periods (horizontal rows) tell you the number of energy levels (shells) holding electrons.
  • Metals are on the left and center (shiny, conduct heat and electricity, tend to lose electrons). Nonmetals are on the upper right (tend to gain or share electrons). Metalloids lie along the staircase line between them.

Families to know:

Group Name Valence electrons Behavior
1 Alkali metals (Li, Na, K) 1 Very reactive; lose 1 electron → +1 ions
2 Alkaline earth metals (Mg, Ca) 2 Reactive; form +2 ions
17 Halogens (F, Cl, Br, I) 7 Very reactive; gain 1 electron → −1 ions
18 Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar) Full outer shell (8; helium 2) Unreactive (inert)

Trends: across a period from left to right, atoms get smaller and electronegativity (pull on shared electrons) increases. Going down a group, atoms get larger and electronegativity decreases. Fluorine is the most electronegative element.

4. Chemical bonds

Atoms bond to reach a stable, full outer shell (the octet rule: 8 valence electrons).

Bond How Usually between Example
Ionic Electrons are transferred; opposite ions attract A metal and a nonmetal NaCl (table salt)
Covalent Electrons are shared Two nonmetals H₂O, CO₂, CH₄
Polar covalent Shared unequally (one atom pulls harder) Nonmetals with different electronegativity The O–H bonds in water
Hydrogen bond A weak attraction between molecules (not a true bond within a molecule) The H of one polar molecule and an O, N or F of another Between water molecules; holds DNA strands together

Check 2: Sodium (group 1) reacts with chlorine (group 17). What type of bond forms, and why? Answer at the bottom of the page.

Common traps

  • Counting electrons in the mass number. Mass number = protons + neutrons only.
  • Confusing isotopes and ions. Isotopes differ in neutrons; ions differ in electrons.
  • Mixing up groups and periods. Groups are columns (similar properties); periods are rows.
  • Thinking a positive ion gained protons. It lost electrons; the proton count never changes in chemical reactions.
