TEAS objective E.3.1 · Using Language and Vocabulary to Express Ideas in Writing

What the TEAS expects: you know what happens at each stage of the writing process and can recognize a strong thesis statement and supporting evidence.

1. The stages

Stage What the writer does
Prewriting (planning) Understand the task and audience; brainstorm, freewrite, or list ideas; narrow the topic; write a working thesis; outline
Drafting Write a full first version, focusing on getting ideas down
Revising Improve the big picture: ideas, organization, evidence, clarity; add, cut, move, or rewrite whole parts
Editing and proofreading Fix surface errors: grammar, spelling, punctuation, capitalization, formatting
Publishing Share or submit the final version

The process loops: revising may send you back to prewriting.

Memory hook: Revising = re-seeing the whole piece; editing = edging out small errors.

Check 1: A writer moves a paragraph and adds an example. Is that revising or editing? Answer at the bottom of the page.

2. Thesis statements

A thesis states the main claim of an essay in one or two sentences. It is:

  • Arguable: someone could disagree (not just a fact). Claims with should or would are usually arguable.
  • Specific: focused enough to support in the essay.
Weak Strong
The TEAS has four sections. (fact) Free TEAS preparation would help more students enter health programs.
Exercise is good. (vague) Our town should add sidewalks along Main Street so older residents can walk safely every day.

3. Supporting evidence

Strong support includes facts, data, examples, expert sources, and reasoning that connect directly to the thesis. Personal opinions alone are weak support.

Citing sources: when you use someone else's words or ideas, give credit. Copying without credit is plagiarism.

Check 2: Which is a thesis: "Nursing is a job" or "Community colleges should expand evening nursing classes for working adults"? Answer at the bottom of the page.

Common traps

  • Mixing up revising and editing. Content and organization = revising; errors = editing.
  • Choosing a fact as a thesis.
  • Skipping prewriting on timed writing; a two-minute outline saves time.
