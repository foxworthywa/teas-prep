Evaluating an Argument

TEAS objective R.3.3 · Integration of Knowledge and Ideas

What the TEAS expects: you can find an author's claim and evidence, judge how strong the evidence is, and spot weak reasoning.

1. Parts of an argument

  • Claim: the main point the author wants you to accept.
  • Reasons and evidence: facts, data, studies, examples, and expert statements that support the claim.
  • Counterargument and rebuttal: an opposing view and the author's answer to it.

Check 1: "Colleges should offer online tutoring all night. At one college, students who used it at least three times were more likely to pass math." What is the claim, and what is the evidence? Answer at the bottom of the page.

2. Strong vs. weak support

Stronger Weaker
Data from a study or survey One person's story (a testimonial)
Expert or official sources Unnamed "experts" or "everyone knows"
Evidence that directly fits the claim Evidence about a related but different topic
Balanced, fair language Insults, exaggeration, overgeneralization

3. Common reasoning problems

  • Overgeneralization: "Every administrator only cares about profit."
  • Personal attack: attacking the person instead of the idea.
  • Testimonial only: "Jamie from Ohio loves it!"
  • Correlation treated as cause: two things happen together, so one must cause the other.
  • Either/or: pretending there are only two choices.

Check 2: "Students who eat breakfast get better grades, so breakfast makes students smarter." What is the reasoning problem? Answer at the bottom of the page.

4. Strengthen or weaken?

To strengthen an argument, add evidence that supports the author's claim or main reason. To weaken it, add evidence that contradicts the claim or shows another cause.

Example: The claim is "Later start times reduce teen car crashes." A study showing fewer crashes after start times changed would strengthen it; a study showing crashes fell everywhere, even where start times stayed the same, would weaken it.

Common traps

  • Agreeing with the claim and then calling the evidence strong.
  • Treating every number as strong evidence. Ask where it came from and whether it fits the claim.
  • Missing the rebuttal. Authors often answer critics with "However…"

Check answers

  1. Claim: colleges should offer all-night online tutoring. Evidence: the finding that, at one college, students who used it were more likely to pass math.
  2. Correlation treated as cause. Other factors (sleep, family support) could explain both.
