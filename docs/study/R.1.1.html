TEAS objective R.1.1 · Key Ideas and Details

What the TEAS expects: you can pick the statement that best sums up a passage of several paragraphs.

1. What a good summary does

A summary is a short restatement of the main idea and the most important supporting points, in your own words. A good summary:

  • covers the whole passage, not just one paragraph;
  • keeps the author's meaning (no new ideas, no opinions of your own);
  • leaves out small details, examples, and numbers unless they are the point.

2. A fast method

  1. Read the first and last paragraphs closely. Main ideas often appear there.
  2. For each paragraph, think of a three- to five-word label. For an article about bees, the labels might be "how bees make honey," "bees pollinate crops," and "bee numbers falling."
  3. Ask: what do all the labels have in common? That is the main idea.
  4. Check each answer choice against the whole passage.

3. How wrong answers are built

Wrong-answer type What it looks like
Too narrow A true detail from one paragraph
Too broad A big claim the passage never makes
Distorted Uses the passage's words but changes the meaning
Contradicts States something the passage rejects

Check 1: A passage has three paragraphs: one on how bees make honey, one on how bees pollinate crops, and one on why bee populations are falling. Is "Bees pollinate many crops" a good summary? Why or why not? Answer at the bottom of the page.

4. Summary vs. main idea vs. theme

  • Main idea: the one point the passage makes (one sentence).
  • Summary: the main idea plus the key supporting points (a few sentences, or one long one).
  • Theme: a lesson about life that a story suggests ("telling the truth brings relief"). Themes are most common in stories; with nonfiction, compare main ideas and values instead (see R.3.2).

Check 2: Which is the better summary of a story in which a girl loses a race, trains all winter, and wins in the spring? (a) "A girl runs in a race." (b) "After losing a race, a girl trains hard and wins the next one." Answer at the bottom of the page.

Common traps

  • Choosing a choice because it quotes the passage. Exact words can still be distorted.
  • Choosing the most interesting detail. A summary is about the whole.
  • Adding your own opinion. "Bees are amazing" isn't in the passage.
