TEAS objective S.4.3 · Scientific Reasoning

What the TEAS expects: you can read a trend in a table or graph, recognize direct and inverse relationships, and use a pattern or a cause-and-effect chain to predict what will happen next.

1. Kinds of relationships

Swipe the table sideways to see every column. →

Relationship As X increases, Y… Graph shape Example
Direct (positive) Increases Line slopes up More exercise → higher heart rate
Inverse (negative) Decreases Line slopes down Higher altitude → lower boiling point; larger gas volume → lower pressure
No relationship Shows no pattern Scattered points or a flat line Shoe size and test scores
Rises then falls Increases to a peak, then decreases Hill-shaped Enzyme activity vs temperature
Levels off Increases, then stops changing Curve that flattens Enzyme reaction rate vs amount of substrate (levels off once all the enzyme is busy)

On a graph, the independent variable (the one changed on purpose) goes on the x-axis; the dependent variable (the result) goes on the y-axis.

2. Making predictions from a pattern

  1. Find the step size or rate of change. Example: boiling point drops 5 °C per 1,500 m of altitude.
  2. Check whether the pattern is steady (same change each step) or changing (doubling, slowing, peaking).
  3. Interpolate (predict between known points) with more confidence than you extrapolate (predict beyond them). Extrapolating assumes the pattern continues, which may not be true.

Doubling pattern: bacteria that double every 20 minutes go 100 → 200 → 400 → 800 in one hour. Not 100 → 200 → 300.

Check 1: A plant grows 2 cm in week 1, 4 cm in week 2, and 6 cm in week 3 (each number is that week's growth). If the pattern continues, how much will it grow in week 4? Answer at the bottom of the page.

3. Cause-and-effect chains

Many TEAS questions ask you to follow a chain of events. Work one link at a time.

Example: a person becomes dehydrated → the blood becomes more concentrated and blood volume falls → the hypothalamus senses this, and ADH is released from the posterior pituitary → the kidneys reabsorb more water → urine becomes less in volume and more concentrated.

Example: more CO₂ in the blood → blood becomes more acidic (pH falls) → the brainstem increases the breathing rate → more CO₂ is exhaled → pH rises back toward normal.

Food chains work the same way: if a predator population falls, its prey population usually rises, and then the plants the prey eat decrease.

4. Reading graphs carefully

  • Read the title, axis labels and units first.
  • Check the scale: does each grid line equal 1, 5 or 10?
  • Find the trend before looking at single points.
  • Answer only from what the graph shows.

Check 2: In a pond, frogs eat insects and snakes eat frogs. If a disease kills most of the snakes, what will most likely happen to the insect population at first? Answer at the bottom of the page.

Common traps

  • Assuming every trend is a straight line. Look for peaks, plateaus and doubling.
  • Swapping the axes. Independent on x, dependent on y.
  • Extrapolating too far. The pattern may change outside the data.
  • Skipping links in a chain. Trace each step.
