Logic and Evidence in Science

TEAS objective S.4.2 · Scientific Reasoning

What the TEAS expects: you can tell observations from inferences, judge which evidence best supports or weakens a claim, recognize that correlation does not prove causation, and choose the conclusion the data actually support.

1. Observation, inference, hypothesis, theory, law

Swipe the table sideways to see every column. →

Term What it is Example
Observation What you directly see or measure "The patient's temperature is 39 °C."
Inference A conclusion drawn from observations "The patient probably has an infection."
Hypothesis A testable, falsifiable prediction (one that a result could prove wrong) "If patients take drug X, their fever will drop faster than with a placebo."
Theory A well-tested, broad explanation supported by much evidence Cell theory, germ theory
Law A description of what always happens, often as an equation; it does not explain why Boyle's law, law of conservation of mass

A theory does not become a law with more evidence; they do different jobs (explaining vs describing).

Check 1: "The leaves are yellow" or "The plant is not getting enough nitrogen": which is the observation and which is the inference? Answer at the bottom of the page.

2. Judging evidence

Stronger evidence usually:

  • comes from a controlled experiment (a control group, one variable changed),
  • uses a large, random sample,
  • is measured, not just reported or felt,
  • has been repeated (replicated) by others,
  • comes from a source without a conflict of interest and is peer reviewed.

Weaker evidence: a single anecdote or testimonial, a tiny or self-selected sample, an advertisement, an opinion, or a result no one has repeated.

Best design for "does treatment X work?": a randomized controlled trial, ideally double-blind (neither patients nor researchers know who got the treatment) with a placebo group (a group given a fake treatment, such as a sugar pill).

3. Inductive and deductive reasoning

  • Inductive: from specific observations to a general rule. "Every swan I've seen is white, so all swans are white." Useful, but a new observation can overturn it.
  • Deductive: from a general rule to a specific conclusion. "All mammals have hair; a whale is a mammal; so a whale has hair." If the premises are true, the conclusion must be true.

4. Correlation is not causation

A correlation means two things change together. It does not show that one causes the other.

  • Third (confounding) variable: ice cream sales and drowning both rise in summer. Hot weather drives both.
  • Reverse causation: people with more hospital visits are sicker, but the illness causes the visits, not the other way around.
  • Coincidence: with enough data, some things line up by chance.

To show causation, change one variable on purpose in a controlled experiment and see whether the other changes.

5. Choosing the right conclusion

On the TEAS, the best conclusion:

  • is supported by the data given, not by outside facts,
  • does not overreach (a study of adults says nothing certain about children; one experiment does not "prove" a hypothesis),
  • matches the strength of the evidence ("suggests" vs "proves").

Watch for answer choices with always, never, proves or all. They often claim more than the evidence shows.

Supporting vs weakening: evidence supports a claim if it is what you would expect if the claim were true. It weakens the claim if it shows the predicted effect doesn't happen, or points to another explanation.

Check 2: A study finds that people who carry lighters are more likely to get lung cancer. A news headline says "Lighters cause cancer." What is the most likely explanation for the link? Answer at the bottom of the page.

Common traps

  • Calling an inference an observation. Words like "probably," "because" and "seems" signal inference.
  • Trusting testimonials. One person's experience is weak evidence.
  • Jumping from correlation to cause. Look for a third variable.
  • Picking the "proves" answer. Experiments support or refute; they rarely prove.
