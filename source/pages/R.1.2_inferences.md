# Making Inferences About a Text

**TEAS objective R.1.2** · Key Ideas and Details

**What the TEAS expects:** you can draw conclusions that a passage supports but doesn't state directly, including conclusions about its purpose and meaning.

---

## 1. What an inference is

Something **implied** is suggested but not said directly. An **inference** is a logical conclusion based on **evidence in the text plus reasoning**. It's not a wild guess, and it's not something stated word for word.

**Example:** *Maya checked the clock for the third time, tapped her foot, and sighed.*
The text never says "Maya is impatient," but the evidence supports that inference.

---

## 2. The evidence test

For each answer choice, ask: **"Which words in the passage prove this?"**

| Choice is… | Why |
|---|---|
| **Correct** | Clearly supported by specific details |
| **Too far** | Might be true, but goes beyond what the text supports |
| **Opposite** | Contradicted by a detail |
| **Unrelated** | About something the passage doesn't discuss |

The right inference is usually **modest**. Watch for extreme words such as *always, never, all, only,* or *proves*.

> **Check 1:** *The parking lot was empty, the lights were off, and a sign on the door read "Back Monday."* Which is the best inference: (a) the store went out of business, or (b) the store is closed for now?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

## 3. Inferring a character's feelings or motives

Look at **actions, words, and reactions**. What a character does often says more than what the narrator states.

*"You noticed something the rest of us missed," the nurse said.* → The nurse values careful observation.

---

## 4. Inferring purpose and meaning

Ask why the author included a detail, a quotation, or an example. What would be lost if it were removed?

> **Check 2:** A health article about handwashing opens with a story about a child who caught the flu from a shared toy. What can you infer about why the author begins this way?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

## Common traps

- **Using outside knowledge** instead of the passage.
- **Picking the most dramatic choice.** Strong inferences are usually cautious.
- **Confusing stated with implied.** If it's stated word for word, it's not an inference question's best answer, but it can still be the evidence.
