# The Author's Purpose

**TEAS objective R.2.3** · Craft and Structure

**What the TEAS expects:** you can tell why an author wrote a text, or why the author included a particular detail, example, or sentence.

---

## 1. The main purposes

| Purpose | Clues | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Inform** (explain or teach) | facts, definitions, neutral tone | textbook section, news report |
| **Persuade** (convince) | opinions, *should*, reasons, a **call to action** (telling the reader to do something) | editorial, ad, letter to the editor |
| **Entertain** (tell a story or amuse) | characters, plot, humor, description | short story, personal narrative |
| **Instruct** (tell how to do something) | numbered steps, commands | directions, recipe, medication label |

Some texts mix purposes; pick the **main** one.

> **Check 1:** A pamphlet lists "five reasons you should quit smoking today" and ends with a phone number for a support line. What is its main purpose?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

## 2. Why is this detail here?

Questions often ask why the author included **one** part. Common answers:

- to give **evidence** for a claim (a statistic, a study);
- to give an **example** that makes an idea concrete;
- to **grab attention** (an opening story, a surprising fact, a question to the reader);
- to answer a **counterargument**;
- to show a **cause and effect** chain.

**Test:** imagine the detail removed. What would the reader lose?

> **Check 2:** An article says oysters filter the water, and the cleaner water lets sunlight reach underwater grasses that shelter young crabs. Why does the author mention the grasses?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

## Common traps

- **Choosing "inform" for every text with facts.** Ads and editorials use facts to persuade.
- **Confusing the topic with the purpose.** "Oysters" is the topic; "to explain why oysters matter" is the purpose.
- **Overreading** a light or funny detail as the main purpose.
