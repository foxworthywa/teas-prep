# Integrating Multiple Sources

**TEAS objective R.3.4** · Integration of Knowledge and Ideas

**What the TEAS expects:** you can combine information from two or more sources, such as an article and a table, directions and a schedule, or two viewpoints, and judge how they fit together.

---

## 1. Read each source for its job

| Source type | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| Article or essay | claims, explanations, advice |
| Table, chart, or graph | numbers and patterns |
| Directions or label | rules and limits |
| Schedule or calendar | when and where |
| Ad or opinion piece | a viewpoint, possibly biased |

Before answering, write a few words for each source: *Source 1: no screens before bed. Source 2: more screen time = less sleep.*

---

## 2. How sources relate

- **Support:** the data back up the article's advice.
- **Contradict:** one source says the opposite.
- **Add:** one source gives a detail the other lacks.
- **Combine:** you need both to answer (for example, a rule from the directions plus an open day from the schedule).

> **Check 1:** Directions say to fast (not eat) for 12 hours before a blood test. The lab opens at 7 a.m. and is open only Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. It is now Tuesday at noon, and a patient wants the earliest possible test. When should she stop eating?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

## 3. What data can and can't show

A survey or table shows **what happened together** (a correlation). By itself, it can't prove that one thing **caused** another. If students who used screens longer slept less, screens *might* be the cause, but stress or late-night jobs could explain both.

> **Check 2:** A table shows that towns with more ice cream sales have more sunburns. Does ice cream cause sunburn?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

## Common traps

- **Answering from only one source** when the question needs both.
- **Treating a correlation as proof of cause.**
- **Trusting a biased source** (an ad) over a neutral one without checking.
