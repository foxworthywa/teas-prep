# Comparing Themes Across Texts

**TEAS objective R.3.2** · Integration of Knowledge and Ideas

**What the TEAS expects:** you can identify the theme or central message of one or more texts and compare how texts treat similar ideas.

---

## 1. What a theme is

A **theme** is the message or lesson about life that a story suggests. It's a **complete thought**, not a single word. With nonfiction, such as opinion pieces, compare **main ideas** and **values** (beliefs about what is important) instead.

- Topic: *failure* (one word)
- Theme: *Failure can teach you how to succeed.* (a lesson)

Find the theme by asking: **What does the main character learn or change? What happens because of it?**

> **Check 1:** In a story, a boy hides a broken window from his parents, feels worse every day, and finally tells the truth and feels relieved. What is the theme?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

## 2. Comparing two texts

| Step | Ask |
|---|---|
| 1 | What is each text's main idea or theme? |
| 2 | Where do they **agree**? (shared theme, shared value) |
| 3 | Where do they **differ**? (focus, attitude, how the character reaches the lesson) |

Two stories can share a theme but reach it differently: one character figures things out alone; another resists advice first.

Two **opinion pieces** can disagree on a solution yet share a value (both care about students), or focus on different concerns (health vs. cost).

> **Check 2:** Passage A argues for later school start times to protect teens' health. Passage B worries that paying for extra buses would take money away from teachers and student programs. What value do both authors share?
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

## Common traps

- **Naming a topic instead of a theme.**
- **Choosing a theme that fits only one of the texts.**
- **Mixing up which passage said what** in comparison questions. Label each passage's main point before reading the choices.
