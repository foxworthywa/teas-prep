# Sentence Structure

**TEAS objective E.1.3** · Conventions of Standard English

**What the TEAS expects:** you can recognize complete sentences, fix fragments and run-ons, identify sentence types, and place modifiers correctly.

---

## 1. What makes a complete sentence

A complete sentence (an **independent clause**) has a **subject** (who or what the sentence is about), a **verb** (the action or state), and expresses a **complete thought**.

A **dependent clause** also has a subject and verb, but it starts with a word like *because, when, although, if*, or *since*, so it can't stand alone. (See the "Words you need" box on the E.1.2 Punctuation page.)

- **Fragment:** missing a subject or verb, or begins with a word like *because, when, although, if, since* without finishing the thought. *Because the clinic was closed.* ✗
- **Not a fragment:** a command such as *Sit down.* The subject "you" is understood.
- **Fix:** attach it to a complete sentence. *Because the clinic was closed, we went home.* ✓

---

## 2. Run-ons and comma splices

- **Run-on:** two complete sentences with no punctuation. *The results came back they were normal.* ✗
- **Comma splice:** two complete sentences joined by only a comma. *The results came back, they were normal.* ✗
- **Four fixes:**
  1. Period: *The results came back. They were normal.*
  2. Semicolon: *The results came back; they were normal.*
  3. Comma + a FANBOYS word (**for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so**): *The results came back, and they were normal.*
  4. Make one clause dependent: *When the results came back, they were normal.*

> **Check 1:** Is this correct? "I finished the test, I felt relieved."

## 3. Four sentence types

| Type | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | One independent clause (may have one subject doing two actions) | The nurse checked the chart and called the doctor. |
| **Compound** | Two independent clauses joined by a comma + FANBOYS word or a semicolon | The nurse checked the chart, and the doctor reviewed it. |
| **Complex** | One independent + at least one dependent clause | Because she checked the chart, the nurse knew the dose. |
| **Compound-complex** | Two independent + at least one dependent clause | When the lab called, the nurse answered, and the doctor listened. |

**Tip:** first count the **independent clauses** (ones that could stand alone), then look for a **dependent clause** (one starting with *because, when, although, if, since*).
- "The nurse checked … and called": **one** subject with two verbs, so one clause: **simple**.
- "The nurse checked the chart, and the doctor reviewed it": two clauses that could each stand alone: **compound**.
- "Because she checked the chart, the nurse knew the dose": the first clause can't stand alone: **complex**.

## 4. Modifiers

A **modifier** is a word or phrase that describes something else (*covered in mud, almost, walking into the room*). It should sit **next to** the word it describes.
- **Dangling modifier:** *Walking into the room, the monitor beeped.* (The monitor wasn't walking.) ✗
- **Fixed:** *Walking into the room, the nurse heard the monitor beep.* ✓
- **Misplaced modifier:** *She almost drove the patient for an hour.* → *She drove the patient for almost an hour.*

> **Check 2:** Fix: "Covered in mud, she washed the dog."
> *Answer at the bottom of the page.*

---

## 5. Parallel structure

Items in a list or pair should have the same grammatical form.
*She likes reading, swimming, and to hike.* ✗ → *She likes reading, swimming, and hiking.* ✓

---

## Common traps

- **Calling a long sentence a run-on.** Length doesn't matter; joining is what matters.
- **Mistaking one subject with two verbs for a compound sentence.** It needs two complete clauses.
- **Starting with a phrase that describes the wrong noun.**
