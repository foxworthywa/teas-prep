## Findings

### S.3.1 Atoms and the Periodic Table

**1. Should-fix (gap): there is no general rule for valence electrons outside groups 1, 2, 17 and 18**
- Quoted: "| 1 | **Alkali metals** ... | 1 |" … "| 18 | **Noble gases** ... | Full outer shell (8; helium 2) |"
- Problem: TEAS items often ask for the valence electrons or likely ion charge of O, N, C, Al and similar elements. The page only gives four families, so a student has no way to work out oxygen's 6 valence electrons and −2 charge. Shell capacity (2, 8, 8 for the first 20 elements) is also missing, even though "energy levels (shells)" is used throughout.
- Suggested addition (under "Families to know"): "For the main groups, valence electrons = the group number for groups 1–2 and the group number minus 10 for groups 13–18 (so oxygen, group 16, has 6). Atoms with 1–3 valence electrons tend to lose them; atoms with 5–7 tend to gain enough to reach 8, so oxygen forms O²⁻ and nitrogen forms N³⁻. The first shell holds up to 2 electrons and the next shells hold 8 each (for the first 20 elements)."

**2. Should-fix (gap): the electronegativity trend down a group is missing**
- Quoted: "across a period from left to right, atoms get **smaller** and **electronegativity** ... **increases**. Going down a group, atoms get **larger**."
- Problem: the page gives only half of the electronegativity trend. "Which is more electronegative, F or I?" and "Cl or Br?" are common TEAS-style questions.
- Suggested replacement: "Going down a group, atoms get **larger** and electronegativity **decreases**. So electronegativity is highest at the upper right (fluorine is the most electronegative element), not counting the noble gases."

**3. Optional: the hydrogen bond is described as only "between molecules"**
- Quoted: "A weak attraction between molecules (not a true bond within a molecule)"
- Problem: hydrogen bonds also form between parts of one large molecule (for example, protein α-helices and β-sheets). Students carry this into A&P and biology, where hydrogen bonds hold protein secondary structure.
- Suggested replacement: "A weak attraction, not a shared or transferred electron. It forms between molecules, or between parts of one large molecule such as a protein."

**4. Optional:** "**Metals** are on the left and center" / "**Nonmetals** are on the upper right." Consider adding "(hydrogen, at the top left, is a nonmetal)." That is a classic trap.

### S.3.2 States of Matter and Physical and Chemical Changes

**5. Optional: plasma is called a "charged gas"**
- Quoted: "**plasma**, is an extremely hot, electrically charged gas"
- Problem: plasma is an *ionized* gas made of charged particles (ions and free electrons). Overall it is roughly neutral.
- Suggested replacement: "an extremely hot gas in which atoms have lost electrons, so it is made of charged particles (ions and free electrons)."

**6. Optional: the "Energy of particles" row**
- Quoted: "| Energy of particles | Lowest | Middle | Highest |"
- Problem: this is fine as a general picture. But the same page correctly says that ice and water at 0 °C stay at the same temperature during melting, so their average kinetic energy is the same there. Consider "(for the same substance; added energy breaks attractions)." Low priority.

**7. Optional:** "Digesting food" is listed under chemical change. Chewing and churning are physical. Consider "Digesting food (enzymes breaking it down)" so that a "chewing = physical" item doesn't confuse students.

### S.3.3 Chemical Reactions and Equations

**8. Optional: the instant cold pack is technically dissolving, not a chemical reaction**
- Quoted: "Instant cold packs and photosynthesis are endothermic." and Check 2 "Is the reaction inside exothermic or endothermic?"
- Problem: a cold pack works by ammonium nitrate *dissolving*, which is an endothermic process. That conflicts slightly with S.3.2, which calls dissolving physical. The endothermic answer is correct, and TEAS uses this example too.
- Suggested replacement for Check 2: "Is the process inside exothermic or endothermic?"

### S.3.4 What Affects How Fast a Reaction Goes

**9. Should-fix (gap, can mislead): no limit on "higher temperature = faster" for enzyme-catalyzed reactions, and pH is not mentioned as a condition**
- Quoted: "| **Temperature** | Higher | **Faster** |" and "**Enzymes** are the body's catalysts; they let reactions run fast at 37 °C."
- Problem: the objective is "how *conditions* affect chemical reactions." A frequent TEAS item asks what happens to an enzyme reaction when temperature (or pH) goes well above its optimum. A student who learned only "higher temperature → faster" will answer wrong. The S.4.3 page does show the rise-then-fall curve, but this page never connects it.
- Suggested addition after the enzymes bullet: "**Exception for enzymes:** each enzyme has an **optimum temperature and pH** (about 37 °C for most human enzymes; about pH 2 for pepsin in the stomach). Above the optimum temperature, or away from the optimum pH, the enzyme **denatures** (loses its shape) and the reaction slows or stops. A high fever can do this."

### S.3.6 Acids, Bases and pH

**10. Optional: "pH runs from 0 to 14"**
- Problem: this is the common teaching range, but very concentrated acids and bases go outside it. Suggested: "pH usually runs from 0 to 14."

**11. Optional: magnesium hydroxide is called a "weak base"**
- Quoted: "a weak base (such as calcium carbonate or magnesium hydroxide)"
- Problem: Mg(OH)₂ is only slightly soluble, but the part that dissolves dissociates fully, so chemistry texts often call it a strong (but sparingly soluble) base. Antacid packaging calls it "mild."
- Suggested replacement: "a mild base (such as calcium carbonate or magnesium hydroxide)". You could also note that carbonate antacids also produce CO₂, which is why they cause burping.

All other content is verified: the litmus colors, the example lists (bleach, ammonia and baking soda are basic; carbonic acid is an acid), strong vs weak, the tenfold rule, blood range and acidosis/alkalosis cutoffs, neutralization, and the bicarbonate buffer with lung and kidney roles.

### S.4.1 Scientific Measurement and Tools

**12. Should-fix (gap, nursing-relevant): micro- is in the table but the step-counting method doesn't reach it**
- Quoted: "**Memory hook (largest to smallest):** **K**ing **H**enry ... **M**ilk ... Each step is a factor of 10." The table lists "**micro-** | μ (or mc in medicine) | 1/1,000,000 of base".
- Problem: King Henry stops at milli. A student who counts "one more step" from milli to micro gets a factor of 10 instead of 1,000. mg ↔ mcg conversions are exactly what pre-nursing students will face.
- Suggested addition: "**Micro** is three places beyond milli (it is not on the King Henry ladder): 1 mg = 1,000 mcg (μg), and 1 g = 1,000,000 mcg. Example: 0.25 mg = 250 mcg."

**13. Should-fix (wording can mislead): tare and subtract**
- Quoted: "**Zero (tare)** a balance before weighing, and subtract the container's mass."
- Problem: taring with the container on the pan already removes the container's mass. "Tare … and subtract" suggests doing both, which double-subtracts.
- Suggested replacement: "**Zero** the balance before weighing. To leave out a container's mass, either **tare** the balance with the empty container on it, or weigh the container separately and subtract its mass. Do one or the other, not both."

**14. Should-fix (gap): no guidance on choosing the more precise tool**
- Quoted: "| Volume of a liquid | **liter (L)** | **Graduated cylinder**, pipette, volumetric flask |"
- Problem: the objective includes "measurement tools." A standard TEAS item asks which glassware gives the most accurate volume. Beakers and Erlenmeyer flasks are never mentioned, so a student can't rule them out.
- Suggested addition: "Beakers and Erlenmeyer flasks have only rough markings. Use them for holding and mixing, not for measuring. For an accurate volume, use a graduated cylinder, or for even better accuracy a pipette, buret or volumetric flask."

**15. Optional:** the section header is "Base units (metric / SI)", but gram, liter and °C are not SI base units (kilogram, cubic meter and kelvin are). The page already flags the kilogram. Consider retitling "Common metric units" to avoid a nitpick question.

### S.4.2 Logic and Evidence in Science

**16. Should-fix (clarity): the reverse-causation example never states the wrong conclusion**
- Quoted: "**Reverse causation:** people with more hospital visits are sicker. The visits did not cause the illness."
- Problem: the example shows the correct reading but not the mistaken causal claim. Students may not see what "reversed" means.
- Suggested replacement: "**Reverse causation:** people who visit the hospital more often are sicker. Concluding that hospital visits make people sick gets the arrow backward: the illness causes the visits."

**17. Optional:** "**Hypothesis** | A testable, falsifiable prediction". More precisely, a hypothesis is a tentative explanation that leads to a testable prediction. The current wording matches common TEAS prep usage and is consistent with S.4.4, so this is low priority.

### S.4.3 Predicting Relationships from Data

**18. Optional: the "Levels off" example doesn't name the x-variable**
- Quoted: "| **Levels off** | ... | Reaction rate as all the enzyme becomes busy |"
- Suggested replacement: "Reaction rate vs substrate concentration (levels off once all the enzyme is busy)." This avoids a reading that the rate levels off over time.

**19. Optional: the ADH chain leaves out the main trigger**
- Quoted: "a person becomes dehydrated → blood volume falls → blood pressure drops → the body releases ADH"
- Problem: the main trigger for ADH is a rise in blood osmolality (the blood becomes more concentrated). Low volume or pressure is a secondary trigger. The chain is not wrong, but the A&P instructor may teach it differently.
- Suggested replacement: "dehydrated → blood becomes more concentrated and blood volume falls → the hypothalamus triggers ADH release → …"
