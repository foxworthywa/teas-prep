## Flagged items

### Substantive flags (should be revised)

**R.3.1-005 (RP-10): ambiguous stem; two options can be defended.** The goal is at least 70% in *every* group, so every group under 70% has to be raised. "Focus on first" can be read two ways. One reading is the largest gap: 18–29, at 28% (B, the likely key). The other is the quickest win: 50–64, at 52%, which is closest to the goal (C). A test-taker who has studied public-health campaigns could also argue for either one. **Fix:** change the stem to "Which group is farthest from the health department's goal?" Or add a criterion, such as "...the group with the lowest vaccination rate."

**R.2.4-001 (RP-05): answer is given away.** Every option begins "As a [role]," and only C names the role the passage gives for Passage B ("parent and bus company owner"). A, a doctor, describes Passage A's author. B (teacher) and D (student) are invented. So the item can be answered by matching labels, without reading Passage B. **Fix:** drop the role prefixes and offer only focus statements, such as "focuses on buses, costs, and schedules," "focuses on how teenagers' bodies respond to sleep loss," "focuses on how schedules affect classroom learning," and "focuses on what students prefer."

- **R.1.3-001 (RP-02):** D is the only option that gives a reason ("because the test may need to be rescheduled"), which makes it stand out. You could give a reason clause to one or two distractors as well, or shorten D to "Call the lab."

- **R.3.1-004 (RP-02):** C's second sentence ("These can change your results") repeats the stem's wording ("can change the results"). That is acceptable for a find-the-evidence item, but it makes the item easy.

The key is also the only moderate option, while A, B and C are all extreme. That is a common testwise cue. It is acceptable, but noted.

## Passage notes

**RP-01 (Yawning):** Accurate. The oxygen experiment matches Provine's 1980s work. The brain-cooling hypothesis (Gallup) and the finding of less yawning in very hot air are represented fairly. So is the stronger contagion among kin (Norscia & Palagi). "Even... fish yawn" is commonly stated and is defensible. No issues.

**RP-02 (Fasting blood test):** Internally consistent: a 12-hour fast, with an 8 p.m. cutoff for an 8 a.m. draw. The content is medically reasonable. No issues.

**RP-03 (Clinic hours):** Consistent. The phone number 555-0142 is in the reserved fictional range, which is good. No issues.

**RP-04 (Allergy label):** Labeled as fictional and realistic in format. The storage range of 68–77 °F matches the standard 20–25 °C. No issues.

**RP-05 (School start times):** The melatonin timing is accurate. During puberty, melatonin onset shifts later (a circadian phase delay), and "about 11 p.m." is the figure the AAP commonly cites. The 8:30 a.m. threshold matches the AAP recommendation. The claims about attendance and fewer crashes are consistent with published district studies. Small point: R.3.3-005 option A says melatonin is released later in teens "than adults'." The passage says only "during puberty... later," but that is a distractor, so it doesn't matter.

**RP-06 (Sugar letter):** "A 20-oz soda contains about 16 tsp of sugar" is correct (about 65 g at 4 g per tsp, which is the CDC's figure). **"Health agencies recommend that most adults limit added sugar to well under 12 teaspoons a day" is muddled.** The Dietary Guidelines limit of under 10% of calories works out to *under about 12 tsp* (50 g) on a 2,000-calorie diet. The American Heart Association's limits are about 6 tsp for women and 9 for men. "Well under 12" blends the two sources. It isn't false, because both are under 12, but a careful reviewer would question it. Items treat this sentence as a factual claim (R.2.1-004 C, R.3.3-003 context). **Suggested fix:** "The American Heart Association recommends that women limit added sugar to about 6 teaspoons a day and men to about 9." Alternatively: "Federal guidelines recommend keeping added sugar under about 12 teaspoons a day for most adults." The phrase "Nothing could be more ridiculous" is fine as the opinion target.

**RP-07 (First Shift):** Fine. As noted above, the story never states that Dana is an "aide," although three item options assume it.

**RP-08 (Nightingale):** The history checks out. She arrived at Scutari (Üsküdar, now Istanbul) in November 1854. Disease deaths, including cholera and typhus (also typhoid and dysentery), far exceeded deaths from wounds. After the war she analyzed the statistics with William Farr. Her "colorful circular charts" were the polar-area ("coxcomb") diagrams of 1858. *Notes on Nursing* is correctly dated 1859 (the first edition appeared in December 1859, though some printings are dated 1860). The Nightingale Training School at St Thomas' opened in 1860. One nuance, not an error: the school was founded through the Nightingale Fund, and she did not run it day to day, so "she opened" is a simplification. The 1858 distractor in R.1.4-002 is fine.

**RP-09 (Chesapeake oysters):** Mostly accurate. The "up to 50 gallons a day" figure is the standard one used by the Chesapeake Bay Foundation and NOAA. The causes of decline (overharvesting, MSX and Dermo disease, pollution) are right, as are 2–3 years to market size and the effects on grasses, crabs and fish. **One factual issue: "Since the 2010s, government agencies, watermen, and volunteer groups have worked to restore oyster reefs" understates the timeline.** Organized restoration dates to at least the 1990s (for example, the Oyster Recovery Partnership was founded in 1994, and agencies planted shell and seed before that). The 2010s mark the large-scale tributary program: the 2014 Chesapeake Bay Watershed Agreement set the goal of restoring 10 tributaries. **Suggested fix:** "For decades... and since 2014, a bay-wide effort has worked to restore reefs in ten rivers." Or simply: "In recent decades, government agencies...". No items depend on the date.

**RP-10 (Flu survey):** Fictional and consistent. The 70% goal matches the Healthy People 2030 target. The type label says "Bar chart data" but the data is given as a table. If no chart is rendered for students, change the label to "Table."

**RP-11 (Parking memo):** Consistent. The form is due January 9, pickup starts January 10, and enforcement starts January 12. Small gap in the wording: "Beginning January 12... must display" and "without a permit *after* January 12... warning" leave January 12 itself, and January 26 itself, unclear. No item tests those dates.

**RP-12 (DreamDeep ad):** Appropriate as a fictional ad. Its false "100% natural, so... completely safe for everyone" claim is the teaching target. The FDA disclaimer is realistic. You could add "(fictional product)" to the body text, as RP-04 does, in case the passage is shown without its title.

**RP-13 (Screens and sleep):** The fictional survey is internally consistent and monotonic. The blue-light and melatonin claim is standard sleep-hygiene advice. Its research support is mixed, but it is fine attributed to "sleep experts." No issues.

**RP-14 (Paired stories):** Fine. The phrase "failed the bridge-building contest" is slightly odd ("lost" or "came in last in" reads more naturally). The reference to Mr. Lyle's late wife is gentle and appropriate.

**RP-15 (Saline rinse):** Medically reasonable: ½ tsp salt in 8 oz of warm water, don't swallow, and seek care for a sore throat lasting more than a week, a high fever or trouble breathing. The step order is unambiguous. No issues.

**RP-16 (Online tutoring essay):** Fine for an argumentative passage. The tutoring statistic is correlational, with a likely selection effect. That makes good material for a future "evaluate the evidence" item, but no current item depends on it.

**Appropriateness:** Nothing in any passage is inappropriate for the test audience. All products, surveys, clinics and counties are presented as fictional or generic.
