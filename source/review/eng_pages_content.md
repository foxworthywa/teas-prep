# TEAS English pages: content audit (E.1.1 to E.3.2)

Reviewer scope: every rule, example, table row, memory hook, word part, Check question and Check answer on the eight English pages, judged against standard American English and against how TEAS 7 items are likely to be keyed. No files were edited.

Overall: no Check answer is wrong, and the medical word parts are almost all correct. The real problems are (a) one page that contradicts its own "be consistent" advice on the serial comma, (b) a few rules or table entries that are true as written but would push a student toward a wrong answer on a TEAS item, and (c) several high-yield gaps, mostly in E.1.2, E.2.1 and E.3.2.

Both linked OpenStax URLs returned HTTP 200 on 2026-11-18 (Writing Guide Handbook; A&P 2e section 1.6, Anatomical Terminology).

Severity key: **must-fix** = wrong, or likely to cause a missed TEAS item / page breaks its own rule; **should-fix** = imprecise or an important gap; **optional** = polish.

## E.1.1 Spelling

**1. Doubling rule vs the page's own word list (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "**Double the final consonant** (one-syllable word, one vowel + one consonant, vowel suffix)" and, in the misspelled list, "**occurrence** (two r's)" and "**benefited**".
- Problem: occur → occurrence doubles even though *occur* has two syllables, and benefit → benefited does *not* double. The rule as written can't explain either word, so the student is left memorizing exactly the contrast the TEAS tests (occurred/occurring, preferred, beginning, admitted vs benefited, traveled, happened).
- Suggested replacement: "**Double the final consonant** when the word ends in one vowel + one consonant, the suffix starts with a vowel, **and** the word is one syllable *or* stressed on the last syllable: stop → stopped; begin → beginning; occur → occurred. Don't double when the stress is earlier (benefit → benefited; travel → traveled in American English) or when there are two vowels (rain → raining). Never double w, x or y (fix → fixing)."

**2. accept / except (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "accept = receive; except = leave out"
- Problem: In current English, *except* is almost always a preposition or conjunction meaning "but" or "excluding." The verb sense "to leave out" exists but is rare. A student holding "leave out" may not recognize "Everyone except the charge nurse left."
- Suggested replacement: "accept = receive or agree to (accept the offer); except = but, excluding (everyone except me)."

**3. to / too / two (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "to = direction"
- Problem: The most common use, the infinitive marker ("to check," "need to go"), is missing. Students who confuse to/too often do so in exactly that slot.
- Suggested replacement: "to = toward, or part of a verb (to walk); too = also or excessive; two = 2."

**4. Gap: plurals beyond -s/-es (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "**Plurals:** add -es after s, x, z, ch, sh"
- Problem: Nothing on -f/-fe → -ves (knife → knives), -o (potatoes, but photos), irregular plurals (child → children, foot → feet), or the Latin/Greek plurals nursing students meet constantly: diagnosis → diagnoses, bacterium → bacteria, vertebra → vertebrae, nucleus → nuclei, criterion → criteria, phenomenon → phenomena. Also quiz → quizzes (doubled z).
- Suggested addition: a short "Irregular and medical plurals" row or table using the examples above.

**5. Gap: commonly confused pairs (should-fix)**
- Problem: The table leaves out several pairs that show up often in TEAS-style items and in clinical writing: **whose / who's**, **lose / loose**, **principal / principle**, **advice / advise**, **breath / breathe**, **complement / compliment**, **weather / whether**.
- Suggested addition: add rows for at least whose/who's (it fits the contraction test already on the page), lose/loose and breath/breathe.

**6. Gap: capitalization (should-fix; applies to E.1.1 and E.1.2)**
- Problem: Capitalization appears only as a word inside the E.3.1 editing row. Conventions items commonly test proper nouns vs common nouns (Eastern Shore Community College vs "a community college"), titles before names (Dr. Lee vs "the doctor"), family words used as names ("Mom said" vs "my mom"), seasons (lowercase), and course names vs subjects (Biology 101 vs biology).
- Suggested addition: a five-row capitalization table on E.1.1 or E.1.2.

**7. i before e (optional)**
- Quoted: "Exceptions: weird, seize, either, neither, foreign"
- Problem: Leaves out the -cie- exceptions (science, species, sufficient, ancient), which break the "except after c" half of the rule.
- Suggested replacement: "...Exceptions: weird, seize, either, neither, foreign; also science, species, ancient."

**8. Silent e (optional)**
- Quoted: "Keep the e before a consonant suffix: hopeful, careless"
- Problem: There are common exceptions that appear on spelling items: argument, truly, judgment (American English), ninth. Words ending in soft -ce or -ge also keep the e before -able/-ous: noticeable, courageous.
- Suggested addition: "Exceptions: argument, truly, judgment; keep the e after soft c/g: noticeable, courageous."

**9. y to i (optional)**
- Quoted: "Keep the y ... after a vowel (played)"
- Suggested addition: "Exceptions: paid, said, laid, day → daily."

**10. Possessive pronouns trap (optional)**
- Quoted: "Possessive pronouns (its, yours, theirs) never take apostrophes."
- Problem: This is correct. But students may overextend it to indefinite pronouns, which do take 's (everyone's, someone's, one's).
- Suggested addition: "...(Indefinite pronouns do: everyone's, someone's.)"

**11. affect / effect (optional)**
- Quoted: "affect is usually a verb (to influence); effect is usually a noun (a result)"
- Correct as written. You could add one line on the exceptions, since nursing students will meet both: "*effect* as a verb = to bring about (effect change); *affect* as a noun = observed emotion (flat affect), common in mental-health charting."

Check 1 (effect) and Check 2 (occurrence) are correct, and so are their explanations.

## E.1.2 Punctuation

**1. Page breaks its own serial-comma advice (must-fix)**
- Quoted (line 14): "Bring your ID, two pencils, and a water bottle." (serial comma)
- Quoted (line 31): "*Bring three things: your ID, a pencil and water.*" (no serial comma)
- Quoted (line 19): "The serial (Oxford) comma ... is standard in academic writing. Some styles leave it out; be consistent."
- Other lists on this page that skip it: line 5 "commas, semicolons, colons, apostrophes and quotation marks"; line 13 "introductory word, phrase or clause"; line 31 "a list, explanation or example." Line 59 *uses* it: "a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a conjunction."
- Problem: The punctuation lesson teaches consistency and then mixes styles, sometimes in near-identical example sentences. An attentive student will think one of the two examples is wrong.
- Suggested replacement: Use the serial comma throughout, since the page calls it the academic standard: "*Bring three things: your ID, a pencil, and water.*"; "commas, semicolons, colons, apostrophes, and quotation marks"; "word, phrase, or clause"; "a list, explanation, or example." Also change line 19 to "before 'and' or 'or' in a list of three or more."

**2. Gap: semicolon with conjunctive adverbs (should-fix, high yield)**
- Quoted: "**Semicolon (;):** joins two complete sentences that are closely related."
- Problem: The most-tested comma-splice pattern is "The test was hard, however, she passed." Nothing on this page or E.1.3 says that however, therefore, consequently and furthermore are **not** conjunctions and can't join sentences with a comma. E.2.3 then teaches these same words as transitions, so a student could produce or accept the splice.
- Suggested addition: "Before **however, therefore, consequently, furthermore** joining two sentences, use a semicolon (or a period) and a comma after: *The test was hard; however, she passed.* A comma alone creates a comma splice." Repeat the point briefly in E.1.3 section 2 and E.2.3 section 3.

**3. Gap: essential vs nonessential elements (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "Around **extra information** that could be removed | Dr. Lee, the new surgeon, starts Monday."
- Problem: Correct, but the page never shows the opposite case. TEAS items often hinge on *not* adding commas around essential information ("The nurse who was on call answered").
- Suggested addition: "No commas around information needed to identify the noun: *The nurse who was on call answered.* (Which nurse? The one on call.) In American usage, *which* usually takes commas, and *that* never does."

**4. Apostrophes: singular nouns ending in s (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "Plural possessive (ends in s) | add **'**"
- Problem: Without a singular-ending-in-s row, students tend to apply the "ends in s, add only '" shortcut to singular nouns (the boss' office, the virus' structure). Most American academic styles (Chicago, APA) prefer the boss's office and James's. AP-style journalism allows James'.
- Suggested addition: a row reading "Singular noun ending in s | usually add **'s** | the boss's office; the virus's spikes (some styles allow James')." Also add joint possession (Maria and Jo's report) as optional.

**5. Quotation marks (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "In American English, **periods and commas go inside** closing quotation marks ... Question marks go inside only if the quotation itself is a question."
- Problem: Correct but incomplete. Colons and semicolons always go **outside**, and exclamation points follow the question-mark rule.
- Suggested replacement: "...Periods and commas go inside; colons and semicolons go outside. Question marks and exclamation points go inside only if they belong to the quotation."

**6. FANBOYS (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "Before **and, but, or, so, yet** (and the other FANBOYS)"
- Problem: The acronym is used but never expanded. "For" and "nor" are the two members students don't know.
- Suggested replacement: "Before a **FANBOYS** conjunction (**f**or, **a**nd, **n**or, **b**ut, **o**r, **y**et, **s**o) joining two complete sentences."

**7. Gap: hyphens (optional)**
- Problem: No hyphen rule, though E.1.3's answer key uses "mud-covered dog." The compound-adjective-before-a-noun rule (a well-known nurse vs the nurse is well known) is a plausible TEAS item.
- Suggested addition: one line on compound adjectives before a noun.

**8. Introductory element (optional)**
- Quoted: "After an **introductory** word, phrase or clause"
- Suggested addition: "(optional after a very short phrase: *In 2025 she graduated* is acceptable)." This prevents students from rejecting a correct answer choice.

**9. Dates example (optional)**
- Quoted: "May 4, 2026, in Norfolk, Virginia"
- Correct, but as a fragment it hides the comma after the state. Suggested replacement: "*On May 4, 2026, in Norfolk, Virginia, the clinic opened.*"

Check 1 and Check 2 answers are correct.

## E.1.3 Sentence Structure

**1. "Count the subject–verb pairs" tip (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "**Tip:** count the subject–verb pairs. 'The nurse checked … and called' is **one** subject with two verbs, so it's simple."
- Problem: A complex sentence also has two subject–verb pairs ("Because *she checked* ..., *the nurse knew* ..."). Counting pairs alone would label it compound. What separates the types is how many pairs are **independent** and how many are **dependent**.
- Suggested replacement: "**Tip:** find each subject–verb pair, then ask whether it can stand alone. Simple = 1 independent; compound = 2+ independent; complex = 1 independent + 1+ dependent; compound-complex = 2+ independent + 1+ dependent. 'The nurse checked … and called' is one subject with two verbs, so it is still one clause: simple."

**2. Compound predicate undefined; related comma error not shown (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "**Mistaking a compound predicate for a compound sentence.**"
- Problem: The term is never defined on the page. The practical consequence also isn't shown: no comma before "and" when it joins two verbs that share one subject. That is a common TEAS distractor.
- Suggested replacement: "**Mistaking a compound predicate (one subject, two verbs) for a compound sentence.** *The nurse checked the chart and called the doctor* takes no comma before 'and.'"

**3. Gap: imperatives and -ing fragments (should-fix)**
- Problem: The definition ("has a subject, a verb...") will lead some students to call "Sit down." or "Take two tablets daily." a fragment. Also missing: the participial-phrase fragment ("Hoping to pass the TEAS.") and the "which" fragment ("Which made her late.").
- Suggested addition: "Commands are complete: the subject *you* is understood (*Take two tablets daily.*). A phrase starting with an -ing word or *which* is often a fragment: *Hoping to pass the TEAS.* ✗"

**4. Optional**
- Sentence types by purpose (declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory) are not covered. Add a single line if space allows.
- Run-on terminology: some textbooks treat a comma splice as a *type* of run-on, while the page presents them as parallel categories. Suggested addition: "(Some books call both 'run-ons.')"

All examples are correct, and so are Check 1 and Check 2 and their answers. Parallel structure is correct and uses the serial comma.

## E.2.1 Grammar for Clear Writing

**1. Pronoun-case table mislabels who/whom (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "| Third person | he, she, they, who | him, her, them, whom |"
- Problem: *who/whom* are relative/interrogative pronouns, not third-person personal pronouns. The table also never teaches how to choose between them, which is the hardest case-choice item.
- Suggested replacement: Split it into "Third person | he, she, it, they | him, her, it, them" and "Who / whom | who | whom." Add the test: "Answer the question with he/him: *(Who/Whom) called?* → *He* called → **who**. *To (who/whom)?* → to *him* → **whom**."

**2. Singular "they": the rule isn't stated and there's no test strategy (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "Singular 'they' is now widely accepted for a person whose gender is unknown or who uses they: *Each student should bring their ID.* Some formal styles still prefer *his or her*."
- Problem: The usage note is accurate (APA 7, Chicago 17 and MLA accept singular *they*). But the section never states the basic agreement rule, singular antecedent → singular pronoun, which is what TEAS agreement items test. Without it, a student shown "Each nurse must renew ___ license" with choices *their / his or her / its* has no way to choose, and test-prep keys have historically favored *his or her*.
- Suggested replacement: "**Rule:** singular antecedent → singular pronoun; plural → plural (*The nurses renewed their licenses*). Singular *they* is now accepted by APA, MLA and Chicago, but some formal styles and older tests prefer *his or her*. On a test, if one choice avoids the problem by making the subject plural (*Students should bring their IDs*), it is the safest answer."

**3. "Myself" (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "**Myself** only reflects back: 'I did it myself.'"
- Problem: The example is an *intensive* (emphatic) use, not a reflexive one, so the rule and its example don't match. The real test is simpler.
- Suggested replacement: "Use **myself** only when *I* is already in the sentence: *I hurt myself; I did it myself.* Not *Maria and myself went* or *Call Maria or myself.*"

**4. Gaps in subject–verb agreement (should-fix)**
- Problem: Three high-frequency cases are missing.
  - Inverted order: *There **are** three patients* / *Here **is** the chart*.
  - The indefinites some, all, none, most and any, whose number depends on the noun after "of": *Some of the water **is**...*, *Some of the patients **are**...*.
  - Titles and amounts: *Ten milligrams **is** the dose*.
- Suggested addition: three table rows with these examples.

**5. Gap: clarity beyond agreement (should-fix)**
- Problem: The objective is "use grammar to *enhance clarity*." Typical items ask for the clearest or most concise revision, but the page has nothing on active vs passive voice (*The nurse gave the medication* vs *The medication was given*) or on wordiness and redundancy (*at this point in time* → *now*; *past history* → *history*). Comparatives (*more better* ✗; *fewer patients* vs *less fluid*) are also missing.
- Suggested addition: a short section 6, "Concise and direct," covering active voice, cutting redundancy, and fewer/less.

**6. Collective nouns (optional)**
- Quoted: "Usually singular in American English | The **staff is** meeting at noon."
- Correct for standard American English. Optionally add: "When members act separately, rephrase: *The staff members disagree.*" Pronoun agreement also follows the verb: *The team made **its** decision.*

**7. Adjective/adverb (optional)**
- Quoted: "*She did **well** on the test* (not *good*). *The patient feels **bad***"
- Correct. Optionally add: "*I feel well* is also correct when *well* means healthy." That stops students from marking it wrong.

**8. Serial comma (should-fix, part of site-wide item)**
- Quoted: "adverbs describe verbs, adjectives or other adverbs"; "**Unclear 'it,' 'this' or 'they.'**" These omit the serial comma, while line 5 of the same page uses it ("..., and avoid unclear pronoun references").

Check 1 (is) and Check 2 (checked) are correct.

## E.2.2 Writing for Your Audience

**1. Table treats first person and commands as informal (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "| Polite requests: *Could you please…* | Commands: *Send me…* |" and "| Third person in reports; no emojis | First person, emojis |"
- Problem: The table implies first person and imperatives are informal markers, but the same page's model answer uses first person in a formal email ("**I will miss class on Thursday...**"), and its professional example is an imperative ("*Start a peripheral IV and infuse normal saline...*"). On an audience item, a student applying the table could wrongly reject an appropriate formal choice.
- Suggested replacement: "| Polite requests to people you're asking a favor of: *Could you please…* | Blunt demands: *Send me…* |" and "| First person is fine in emails and letters; reports and research writing usually use third person; no emojis | Emojis, casual first person |". Add a note: "Clear instructions (*Take with food*) are appropriate in professional writing."

**2. Gaps for E.2.2 (should-fix)**
- Problem: "Evaluate if language meets the needs of an audience for a provided **rhetorical context**" also covers **purpose** (to inform, persuade, instruct, entertain) and **word connotation** (*thrifty* vs *cheap*, *noncompliant* vs *chose not to*). The page only mentions purpose in one question ("Why am I writing?").
- Suggested addition: a two-row purpose table and a two-line connotation/denotation note with a clinical example.

**3. Optional**
- "Precise words: *support, demonstrate, significant*": in science writing, *significant* implies statistical significance. Consider replacing it with *substantial* or *indicate*.
- Check 1 model answer: consider adding a greeting and sign-off ("Dear Professor Smith, ... Thank you, Alex"), since format is part of what makes an email formal.
- Person-first language: correct as hedged ("or preferred language"). Optionally note that some communities prefer identity-first language (Deaf, autistic person).

## E.2.3 Building a Well-Organized Paragraph

**1. "although" listed as a transition (must-fix)**
- Quoted: "| Contrast | however, on the other hand, although |"
- Problem: *Although* is a subordinating conjunction. It begins a dependent clause inside one sentence and can't link two separate sentences the way the other words in this table do. The page's own Check 2 has exactly that format ("Many students fear the math section. ___, regular practice builds confidence."), and "Although, regular practice builds confidence" would be a fragment-producing error. A student using this table on a TEAS fill-in-the-blank item could pick it.
- Suggested replacement: "| Contrast | however, on the other hand, in contrast, nevertheless |" plus a note: "(*Although* and *while* also show contrast but join clauses inside one sentence: *Although many students fear math, practice builds confidence.*)"

**2. Transition punctuation (should-fix, ties to E.1.2 item 2)**
- Problem: Nothing says that these transitions, when they join two sentences, need a period or a semicolon before them.
- Suggested addition under the table: "Joining two sentences with *however* or *therefore*? Use a period or semicolon before it and a comma after: *...section; however, practice helps.*"

**3. Serial comma (should-fix, part of site-wide item)**
- Quoted: "details, examples, evidence and explanation" (no serial comma), while line 5 uses one ("..., choose transitions, and spot sentences...").

Both Check answers are correct. The order table is accurate.

## E.3.1 The Writing Process

## E.3.2 Word Parts

**1. pneum/o (should-fix)**
- Quoted: "| pulmon/o, pneum/o | lung | pulmonary, pneumonia |"
- Problem: *pneum/o* (and *pneumat/o*) also means **air or breath** (pneumothorax = air in the chest cavity, not "lung chest"). *Pneumon/o* is the lung form. A TEAS-style decoding item on pneumothorax would be missed.
- Suggested replacement: "| pulmon/o, pneumon/o | lung | pulmonary, pneumonia |" and "| pneum/o | air, lung | pneumothorax |"

**2. Word parts used but never defined (should-fix)**
- Problem: The page's examples use parts the student can't decode from the tables:
  - *glyc* (hypoglycemia; it's glossed inline but not in the root table)
  - *-emia* (anemia, hypoglycemia)
  - *arthr/o* (arthritis)
  - *col/o* (colostomy, colonoscopy)
  - *trache/o* (tracheotomy)
  - *therm/o* (hypothermia)
  - *cost/o* (intercostal)
  - *ven/o* (intravenous)
  - *nat/o* (prenatal)
  - *append-* (appendectomy)
  - *endo-* (endoscope)
  - *-tension* (hypertension)
- Suggested addition: Add at least arthr/o (joint), col/o (colon), trache/o (trachea), glyc/o (sugar), therm/o (heat), cost/o (rib), ven/o (vein) to the roots table and -emia (blood condition) to the suffixes table.

**3. High-yield gaps (should-fix)**
- Prefixes: **ab- / ad-** (away from / toward; abduction/adduction, a classic confusion pair), **dys-** (difficult, painful: dysphagia), **endo- / exo-**, **epi-** (upon: epidermis), **hemi- / semi-** (half), **bi- / tri- / poly- / mono-**, **micro- / macro-**, **contra-** (against), **trans-** (across).
- Roots: **cyt/o** (cell), **my/o** (muscle), **path/o** (disease), **hem/o** (already present), **cephal/o** (head), **thromb/o** (clot), **leuk/o / erythr/o** (white/red).
- Suffixes: **-osis** (abnormal condition), **-pathy** (disease), **-plasty** (surgical repair), **-penia** (deficiency), **-cyte** (cell), **-rrhea** (flow).
- Non-medical, since the objective is general vocabulary: **dis-, in-/im-/il-/ir-** (not), **non-, de-, pre-, inter-, trans-**; roots **port** (carry), **dict** (say), **spec/spect** (look), **graph** (write), **chron** (time).

**4. anemia example (optional)**
- Quoted: "| a-, an- | without, not | anemia, asymptomatic |"
- Problem: This is correct morphologically, but anemia literally means "without blood," which is not its meaning (too few RBCs or too little hemoglobin). It's a good teaching moment rather than an error. Suggested addition: "(Word parts give a clue, not always the exact clinical meaning: anemia = literally 'without blood,' actually too few red blood cells.)"

**5. Suffix form (optional)**
- Quoted: "-ostomy", "-otomy"
- The standard suffixes are -stomy and -tomy. The "o" is the combining vowel from the root (col/o + -stomy). The current listing is common in study guides and won't cause a wrong answer. Leave it as is, or note it.

**6. Serial comma (should-fix, part of site-wide item)**
- Quoted: title "Prefixes, Roots and Suffixes"; "its prefix, root and suffix"; "Confusing -ectomy, -otomy and -ostomy." Footer: "Linked textbooks: OpenStax, Writing Guide with Handbook and Anatomy and Physiology 2e" (also slightly ambiguous; suggest "OpenStax *Writing Guide with Handbook* and *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*").

All other word parts and examples are correct: brady-, tachy-, hyper-, hypo-, inter-, intra-, peri-, post-/pre-, sub-, cardi/o, derm/o, gastr/o, hem/o, hepat/o, nephr/o, ren/o, neur/o, oste/o, -algia, -ectomy, -itis, -logy, -megaly, -ostomy, -otomy, -scope/-scopy, and the everyday parts. Check 1 (enlargement of the liver) and Check 2 (under the skin) are correct.

## Site-wide: serial comma consistency (should-fix)

E.1.2 tells students the serial comma is "standard in academic writing ... be consistent." The pages use it in some lists and skip it in others. The places that use it are E.1.1 line 5; E.1.2 lines 14 and 59; E.1.3 lines 5 and 61; E.2.1 line 5; and E.2.3 line 5. Many other lists skip it, including the shared "Learn it properly" label on every page: "**Grammar, punctuation and style handbook**." **Recommendation:** adopt the serial comma everywhere, because APA, MLA and Chicago all use it and the punctuation page endorses it. That means changing the shared label to "Grammar, punctuation, and style handbook" and the E.3.2 title to "Prefixes, Roots, and Suffixes." All specific instances are listed under each page above.

Optional style point: the headings "Formal vs informal" and "Adjectives vs adverbs" use "vs" without a period. American style guides generally write "vs."
